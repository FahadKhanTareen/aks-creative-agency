What drives the cost of a projection mapping show

Where the budget goes in a projection mapping show: content, projectors, rigging, crew and hidden costs, plus a checklist for comparing client quotes.

BY FAHAD KHAN TAREENOCTOBER 16, 20266 MIN READ
AKS FIELD NOTES← All articlesView projects ↗

The first question most clients ask us is how much a projection mapping show costs, and it is the one question we cannot answer honestly without knowing the building. Two shows that look similar in a brochure can differ enormously in price because the cost is not driven by the length of the film. It is driven by site conditions, equipment scale, content complexity and the amount of time the crew needs on site. This note explains where the budget actually goes, so that a production manager can compare quotes sensibly and decide where to spend and where to save.

Why there is no standard price

A projection mapping show is a bespoke production, closer to a stage build than to a software licence. The building is the screen, and every building is different: its size, its surface texture and colour, how far the audience can stand from it and how much ambient light surrounds it. A small, pale, smooth facade viewed from a nearby plaza is a very different job from a large, dark, deeply textured monument that must be seen from far away.

When a supplier gives you a flat figure before seeing the site, either the assumptions are hidden or the scope is thin. A credible quote starts with questions: which surface, which viewing area, how many nights, what content length and what happens around the show. That is not evasion. It is the only way to price the real job.

The main cost drivers

We group the budget into five areas. The proportions vary by project, but almost every show contains all of them.

1. Content creation

Content is design, animation and rendering time, and it scales with the length of the film, the visual complexity and the number of revisions. Fully original 3D animation with simulation and custom effects takes far longer than motion graphics built on existing assets. Content is also tied to the surface: it needs an accurate model or scan of the building, because artwork that does not follow the architecture breaks the illusion. We cover this in more detail in our note on designing projection content for a building facade.

2. Projection hardware

The number and brightness of projectors depend on the surface area to be covered, the material, the colour of the building and the ambient light. A bigger or darker surface needs more light output, and more projectors mean more lenses, more rigging, more cabling and more media server capacity. Our guide to projector brightness explains why lumens alone are a poor way to compare proposals, and our note on projector placement shows how throw distance and positions change the equipment list.

3. Site, rigging and power

Equipment must stand somewhere stable and safe. Scaffold towers, platforms, weather protection, generators or dedicated power, cable runs and crowd barriers can add up to a significant part of the budget, especially at heritage sites where fixings are restricted. Outdoor shows also need contingency for conditions, as we describe in our guide to weather-proofing outdoor projection mapping.

4. Crew and time on site

Alignment, calibration and rehearsal take time, and the setup is rarely shorter than people expect. Crew size and the number of days on site, including load-in, programming, rehearsal, show nights and load-out, affect the price directly. Night-only access for the building often compresses the working window and pushes the schedule longer.

5. Audio, show control and extras

Sound, lighting, lasers, fireworks or special effects are often added to the brief after the visuals are agreed. Each needs equipment, a designer, a controller and rehearsal time. Synchronising them is a task in itself, which is why we treat show control and timecode as part of the design rather than an afterthought. Outdoor audio in particular needs its own plan, as covered in our note on sound for outdoor projection mapping shows.

The costs people forget

Several items regularly appear late in a project and surprise the client. They are worth listing at the start:

  • Permits, site fees and permissions from building owners or authorities, which differ by venue and city.
  • Security, crowd management, first aid and traffic arrangements around the event.
  • Transport, accommodation and local logistics, particularly for events outside the studio's city.
  • Insurance and any liability requirements set by the venue.
  • Spare equipment and an on-site operator for backups.
  • Photography and filming of the show for the client's own use, which is often more valuable than people expect.
  • Time for approvals. Slow sign-off on content is one of the biggest causes of rushed, expensive final weeks.

Where you can sensibly save

Savings that do not harm the result usually come from decisions, not from cutting corners on equipment. A shorter film that is polished and well timed generally has more impact than a long one that is thin. Reusing a single strong visual language across the whole film is cheaper than designing many unrelated scenes. A single well-chosen night of hero programming can be better value than many repeated shows if the audience is concentrated.

Choosing the viewing position early also helps. If the audience is guided to the best angle, we can design for that view instead of covering every possible position, which can reduce both content and hardware. Early access for a site survey, even a short one, reduces risk for everyone, because many of the expensive surprises are found during survey rather than on show night. If you can, allow a previsualisation stage as well; our note on a practical previsualization workflow explains how a digital twin lets the client approve composition before heavy rendering begins.

Where cutting costs backfires

Some savings cost more later. Skipping the site survey, shrinking the rehearsal window, using too few projectors for a dark or large surface and dropping backup equipment all raise the chance of a failure in front of the audience. Last-minute content changes are another trap: each change after rendering has begun can mean re-rendering, re-testing and re-aligning, which costs both money and sleep. We would rather reduce scope openly than quietly weaken the parts that protect the show.

A checklist for comparing quotes

Ask each supplierWhy it matters
Is a site survey included, and when?Quotes made without a survey rest on assumptions.
How many projectors, and at what brightness?Shows whether the surface and ambient light were considered.
How many content revisions are included?Prevents surprise charges during approvals.
How many days on site and how many crew?Reveals whether rehearsal time is realistic.
What backup equipment is on site?Determines how a failure is handled on the night.
Are power, rigging and permits included?These items are often excluded and added later.
Who owns the final content files?Affects reuse, archive and future events.

How to brief a studio so the quote is useful

The more precisely you describe the project, the closer the first quote will be to the final one. Send photographs or drawings of the building from the audience position, the date and time of the event, the expected audience size and where people will stand. Tell us the story or message, the approximate length of the film and any other elements, such as music, lighting or speeches, that must be in the same programme. Mention constraints such as heritage rules, noise limits, access hours and whether the show repeats.

Share your budget range too. It is easier to design the right show for a stated range than to guess, and a studio that knows the ceiling can propose options at different levels rather than a single take-it-or-leave-it figure. We have worked on landmark sites across Pakistan, including the Marka-e-Haq monument, the ICC launch at the Pakistan Monument and the Mohatta Palace, and on each the early conversation about site, scale and schedule decided the budget more than any single piece of equipment.
