What to send a studio before a projection mapping quote

Which drawings, photos, site details and approvals to send a studio so your projection mapping quote is accurate, comparable and free of padding.

BY FAHAD KHAN TAREENOCTOBER 21, 20267 MIN READ
AKS FIELD NOTES← All articlesView projects ↗

The first message a studio receives about a projection mapping show is usually short: a building name, a date and a question about price. It is a fair place to start, but it is not enough to quote from. A quote built on guesses has to carry the cost of every guess, and the differences between a good and a poor estimate usually come from information the client already has or can gather in an afternoon. At AKS we are regularly asked what we need to see before we can give a realistic proposal. This note sets out the pack of information that makes a studio's first reply faster, more accurate and less padded with contingency.

Why the brief decides the quote

Projection mapping is priced from the surface and the setting, not from a catalogue. The size and texture of the facade decide how many projectors are needed. The distance to a workable projector position decides lens choices and rigging. The audience area decides how much of the building is worth lighting at all. When these facts are missing, a studio has two options: ask a long list of questions, or price defensively. Neither helps a client comparing several proposals, because the defensive quote looks expensive and the optimistic one hides risk.

A good information pack also protects the client. When every studio is quoting from the same documents, the proposals can be compared line by line, and gaps in scope show up before a contract is signed rather than on site.

The building: drawings, photographs and access

The most useful single item is a set of scaled elevations of the surface, ideally as vector drawings or CAD files showing every window, cornice, column and recess. If drawings do not exist, say so. Many heritage and older buildings have no reliable drawings, and the studio will need to plan a survey, which is a normal part of the work and should be budgeted as such.

Alongside drawings, send photographs. Daytime shots from several angles show materials, colour and depth. Night shots show what the surface looks like with its existing lighting on, which matters because the building is the screen. Pale stone, glass, dark brick and painted plaster all behave differently under projection, and a studio can usually judge a lot from a few honest photographs. Include a photograph from where the audience will stand if you know it.

Tell us about the practical constraints at the same time:

  • Which sides of the building can be used, and which are off limits.
  • Any protected or fragile surfaces where nothing may be attached or touched.
  • Whether the existing lighting can be switched off for the show and for the rehearsals.
  • Whether windows will be occupied, curtained or lit during the event.

The site: where equipment and people can go

A projector needs a position, a power source and protection from the weather and the crowd. We ask clients for a simple site plan or a satellite image marked with the building, the likely audience area, the nearest roads and the possible equipment positions. If there are trees, flagpoles, overhead cables, fountains or tents between the building and the places where projectors could stand, mark them. Our notes on projector placement and audience sightlines explain why these obstructions matter so much, and they are far cheaper to discover on a plan than on the second night of rehearsal.

Power and access are the next questions. Is mains power available near the equipment positions, or will generators be needed? When can trucks and crew enter the site, and until what hour? Is there a rigging platform, a rooftop or a scaffold already in place? Even rough answers help, and "we do not know" is a perfectly acceptable answer if it is stated plainly, because it tells the studio what to put in the survey scope.

The event: dates, audience and show format

Next, describe the event itself. The studio needs the show date, the number of shows and how long each one runs. It needs to know how many people are expected and how they will arrive, since a seated audience of a few hundred and a passing crowd of thousands call for very different viewing positions. Mention whether the show is a loop that runs for several evenings or a single scripted performance, and whether it will be tied to a speech, a countdown or a reveal.

Tell us also what time the show can start. Projection needs darkness, and the working window changes by season and location. A show that must run in early evening while the sky is still bright needs more brightness than one that starts after full dark, and that difference can change the equipment list. If the date is fixed, say whether it is fixed by an external event or whether it can move. Flexibility on dates is one of the cheapest ways to reduce schedule risk.

The creative: message, assets and approvals

Clients often assume the creative direction has to be settled before a quote, and it does not. What helps is a clear statement of the purpose of the show, the audience and the tone, along with any material that must appear: logos, brand guidelines, names, key messages, existing film or music. Send logos as vector files where possible, and tell us who owns the music and whether it is licensed for public performance.

Approvals deserve as much attention as assets. Who signs off on the storyboard, the animatic and the final render? How many rounds of feedback are expected, and how quickly can each round be returned? A show with one decision maker and two review rounds is a different production from a show with five stakeholders and open-ended notes. We would rather know this on day one than discover it in week three. Our note on show-week scheduling explains how late changes compress the time left for alignment and rehearsal.

Permissions, budget and what counts as success

Public projection normally involves some form of permission from the building owner, and often from local authorities, traffic management or event organisers. Tell the studio what is already in place and what is still open, and who is responsible for each item. Studios can advise on what is typically needed, but the client usually has to hold the relationship with the venue and the authorities.

Finally, share a budget range if you have one. Some clients hold it back for fear of being quoted up to the limit, but in practice a range lets a studio propose the best show that fits, and tell you honestly when the ambition and the budget do not match. If the figure is not yet fixed, give an order of magnitude and any approval deadlines. Our guide to what drives the cost of a show explains how the number is built, which makes it easier to judge a proposal when it arrives.

It is also worth stating what success looks like. A launch that must photograph well for social media, a civic event that must be seen from a long distance, and a ticketed experience with a seated audience each point toward different choices.

What if you do not have all of it

Most clients will not. Few have scaled drawings, a final date and a confirmed budget on the same day, and a studio that insists on all of it before replying is not being helpful. Send what you have, say what is unknown, and expect the studio to propose a survey or a discovery stage to close the gaps. At AKS we would rather begin with an honest list of unknowns than a confident number that has to be revised later, and we have planned shows at places such as Parliament House in Islamabad, Mohatta Palace in Karachi and Minar-e-Pakistan in Lahore that began exactly that way.
