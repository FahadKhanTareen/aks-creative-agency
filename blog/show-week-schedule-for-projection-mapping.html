A show-week schedule for projection mapping

A day-by-day schedule for projection mapping show week: load-in, alignment, content review and rehearsal, plus a checklist to keep the build on time.

BY FAHAD KHAN TAREENOCTOBER 20, 20267 MIN READ
AKS FIELD NOTES← All articlesView projects ↗

Most projection mapping shows are won or lost in the last week, not on the night. The content may be finished and the equipment may be on the truck, but the show still has to be fitted to a real building, in real darkness, with a real audience arriving on a fixed date. Clients and production managers often ask us how long they need on site and what actually happens in that time. At AKS we plan show week as a sequence of gates, each one proving something before the next begins. This note sets out the schedule we work from, how long each stage tends to take, and where schedules usually slip.

Why show week needs a schedule at all

Projection mapping can only be aligned and judged in the dark, which means the useful working hours are short and fall at the end of the day, often the same hours that other trades, security and event staff want the site. Daytime hours are not wasted, but they are used for rigging, cabling, networking and power checks rather than for the image. If these tasks are not sequenced, crews end up aligning projectors that are still being moved, or reviewing content on a surface that has not been calibrated. A written schedule makes the dependencies visible and gives the client clear points at which to give feedback.

The exact length of the week depends on the size of the surface, the number of projectors, the access to the site and the number of shows. A single building with a modest rig can be fitted in far less time than a multi-building site with scaffolding towers, and nothing below should be read as a fixed rule. It is the order of work that matters, and that order is stable across almost every job we plan.

Before load-in: the paper gates

The first gate happens before any equipment moves. We want the site access and power plan confirmed in writing, the rigging positions agreed, the safety and permission paperwork in hand, and the content checked against the model of the building. If the show was previsualised, this is when the previs is compared with the final plan and any drift is corrected. We also confirm who on the client side has authority to approve changes, because a show week with three people giving opposite notes is a slow one.

It helps to agree the freeze points now. Content should be locked, or at least limited to agreed changes, by a stated time before the first full run. Late content is the most common reason for a show week to run out of hours, and the cheapest way to prevent it is to decide in advance how late a change can be and what it costs in rehearsal time.

Day one: load-in, rigging and power

The first day is physical. Projector stands or platforms go in at the planned positions, cabling runs are laid and protected, the media servers and network are racked and powered, and the weather protection is installed. We check that every projector powers up, receives a picture and sits on a stable platform before we leave the site. A projector that moves after alignment can undo hours of work, so we treat stability as a pass or fail check, not a detail.

This is also the day for a first survey of the site as it really is. Cable lengths, sightline obstructions, reflections from glass and any lighting that cannot be switched off tend to look different in person than on a drawing. Finding them on day one leaves time to respond. Finding them at sunset on show night does not.

Day two: coarse and fine alignment

Once the rig is stable and darkness arrives, the work moves to the image. We begin with a coarse pass: lens shift, focus and zoom set for each projector so that its output covers its intended part of the surface with the right overlap. Only then do we move to the fine pass, where each output is warped and masked in the media server so that edges of the content land on the edges of the architecture. Overlaps between neighbouring projectors are blended so that the seam is not visible.

Alignment is easiest to check with simple test patterns, such as grids, outlines of windows and cornices, and flat colour fields. We view them from the main audience position, not only from the projector position, because a surface with depth can look correct from one angle and wrong from another. At the end of the night the calibration is saved and backed up, and the machine is left in a state that another operator could resume from. If a projector is bumped later, we want to correct that output alone rather than begin again.

Day three: content on the surface

With alignment saved, the real content goes up for the first time. This is the first moment in which the creative team sees how their work behaves on the actual building, and some adjustments are almost always needed. Fine details may vanish at distance. Contrast that looked strong in a studio can look flat against a pale or lit surface. Colours shift when they meet stone, glass or paint. We review the show in sections, take notes against timecodes and push revisions to the server in batches, so that the next pass is not delayed by one change at a time.

This is the day when the client sees the work and gives feedback, so it is worth having the decision makers present for part of it. We prefer a short, structured review of each section to a long conversation at the end, and we keep a single list of notes, owned by one person, so that requests are not lost or duplicated.

Day four: sync with sound, light and effects

Once the picture is settled, the show is locked to everything else. Audio, lighting, any pyrotechnic or special effects, and the media server are brought onto the same show clock, usually through timecode or a shared trigger, and the full running order is played end to end. Our note on show control covers this in more detail. In show week the key tasks are to confirm that cues land where they should, that audio delay at the audience position is accounted for, and that the system recovers cleanly if a signal drops. We deliberately interrupt the show during this stage to see what the operator and the system do, because a recovery that has never been tested is a guess.

Final rehearsals and the dress run

The last rehearsals are run as if for the public. The same people sit at the same desks, the same cue calls are used, and the site is arranged as it will be on the night, including crowd areas and any lights that will be on. The dress run should happen at the same hour as the show, in the same conditions as far as they can be matched, because ambient light and weather will change how the image reads. We use it to confirm timings, check safe places for the operators, and rehearse the fallback plan, such as a holding image or a restart procedure.

Whatever time remains after the dress run is a buffer, not a bonus. We hold at least some of it back for the unexpected, because a show week with no slack turns every small fault into a missed show.

A show-week checklist

StageWhat must be true before moving on
Before load-inAccess, power, permissions, rigging positions and content freeze point agreed in writing
Load-inEvery projector stable, powered and showing a picture; network and weather protection in place
AlignmentWarping, masking and blending checked from the audience position; calibration saved and backed up
Content reviewEach section reviewed on the surface; notes held in one list with one owner
Show syncAudio, lighting and effects locked to one clock; recovery from a dropout tested
Dress runFull run at show time in show conditions, fallback plan rehearsed, buffer time kept

Where schedules usually slip

In our experience the same few things cause most delays. Content arrives after the freeze point. Rigging positions are changed late and invalidate earlier measurements. The site is not dark enough, or not dark for long enough, because other lighting has not been controlled. Access is restricted during the hours the team needs most. Weather interrupts outdoor work, which is why our note on weather-proofing is worth reading alongside this one. None of these is exotic, and most can be reduced by writing down who owns each decision and by building in slack from the outset.
