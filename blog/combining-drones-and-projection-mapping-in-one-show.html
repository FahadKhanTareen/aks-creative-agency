Combining drones and projection mapping in one show

How drones and projection mapping can share one show: roles, airspace, timing, sightlines, wind and safety, with a planning table and checklist.

BY FAHAD KHAN TAREENOCTOBER 30, 20266 MIN READ
AKS FIELD NOTES← All articlesView projects ↗

A drone formation rising in front of a lit building is one of the most requested ideas in live spectacle, and one of the easiest to under-plan. Drones and projection mapping look like natural partners, since both turn the night sky and the architecture into a canvas. In practice they are two separate production systems with different regulators, different risks and different tolerances for weather. This note sets out how we think about combining them: what each does best, how to divide the show, and what to settle before anyone is booked.

What each medium does best

Projection mapping fills a physical surface with a high-resolution image that follows the architecture. It is precise, it can change completely from one second to the next, and it can show detailed motion, texture and typography. Its weakness is that it only exists where there is a surface to receive it. It cannot draw in open air.

A drone formation is the opposite. Each aircraft is a single point of light, so the picture is coarse compared with a projector, and shapes take time to form and dissolve. What drones offer is volume: light that sits in the air, moves in three dimensions and extends well beyond the building. They can frame a structure, trace its outline from a distance, or carry a form from the sky down toward the façade.

The strongest combined shows use each for what it is good at. The building carries the detail and the story, and the drones extend and punctuate it. A show that asks drones to do detailed imagery, or asks projection to fill the sky, will disappoint on both counts.

Deciding who leads each scene

We find it useful to assign every scene a lead medium on the storyboard, and treat the other as support. A scene led by projection might have drones holding a slow, simple shape as a backdrop. A scene led by drones might leave the building in a quiet, dark or low-key state so the formation reads clearly. Our note on storyboarding a projection show covers the method, and the same board can carry a drone layer.

Moments where both are at full intensity are rare and should be chosen. A bright projected façade raises the ambient light around the building, and a formation of small points can lose contrast against it. A short, deliberate handover between the two usually reads better than constant overlap. The colour and contrast choices in the projected content matter here, because a darker or more restrained palette gives the aircraft room to be seen.

Airspace and approvals come first

Drone flights in front of a crowd are regulated by the national aviation authority, and the rules, approvals and pilot qualifications vary by country and by site. We do not treat this as a late paperwork task. Permission to fly over or near a public gathering can be the longest lead item in the schedule, and it can decide whether the idea is possible at all in a given location. Sites near airports, government buildings or restricted zones are likely to carry additional limits.

The drone operator is normally a specialist who holds the approvals and carries the insurance, and the flight plan should be agreed with them in writing before creative work begins. Projection also has its own approvals, as described in our note on permissions for public projection shows. It is worth keeping both on one tracker so that a missing drone approval does not surface in the last week.

Site layout, sightlines and safety zones

A drone show needs a launch and landing area, a flight volume and a safety margin between the aircraft and the audience. Those requirements compete with projector positions, cable runs and viewing areas for the same ground. We map them together on a single site plan before settling any of them.

The audience position then has to work for both media. A projection looks best from a defined viewing zone in front of the building, while a drone formation is usually designed to be read from a particular direction and distance. If the best view of the building and the best view of the formation are in different places, one of them will be compromised. Our note on audience sightlines explains how to define the area, and the drone designer should work from the same plan.

Projector throw is another conflict. Light travelling from a projector to the façade passes through the space in front of the building, which may be exactly where the aircraft are meant to fly. Rigging that sits in the flight volume, such as tall truss or lifting equipment, is a collision risk and has to be shown to the drone team early. For the projection side, see our notes on projector placement and rigging.

Timing and show control

To feel like one show, the two systems must share a clock. Drone shows are typically sequenced against a fixed soundtrack and run by their own flight software, and the projection is driven by a media server. The practical solution is to give both the same reference, usually a shared timecode or a coordinated start cue, and to agree who calls each go. Our guide to show control and timecode describes the approach on the projection side.

A key difference is that a drone show has hard stop conditions for safety. If the operator needs to hold or land the formation, the projection must be able to continue gracefully without it, and the reverse should also be true. We write down what each system does if the other is paused, and we rehearse those cases rather than assuming they will work. This is the same thinking behind our note on backup and redundancy.

Wind, weather and the fallback plan

Small aircraft are more sensitive to wind and rain than a projection rig is, and they can be cancelled by conditions that would not stop a projection. Flight limits are set by the operator and the manufacturer, and they should be asked for directly rather than assumed. A show that depends on drones for its finale should always have a version that still works without them.

We recommend building the show in a way that the drone scenes can be cut or shortened without damaging the story. Our note on weather-proofing outdoor shows covers the projection side, and a shared go or no-go decision time, agreed with the operator in advance, avoids arguments on the night.

A combined-show checklist

  • Each scene has a lead medium, and the support medium is simple and restrained.
  • Aviation approval has been started early and the flight plan is agreed in writing.
  • Projector positions, launch area, flight volume and audience zone are on one site plan.
  • No rigging or lifting equipment sits inside the flight volume.
  • Both systems share a timecode or an agreed start cue, with a named person calling go.
  • The show still works if the drone scenes are shortened or cancelled.
  • The operator has stated wind and rain limits, and a go or no-go time is agreed.
  • Both teams have rehearsed together on site, not only separately.

Drones and projection can make a striking pair when they are planned as one show rather than two bookings that happen to share a night.
