Power and signal cabling for outdoor projection mapping

How to plan power, generators, UPS, signal runs and cable protection for outdoor projection mapping shows, with a pre-show checklist for production managers.

BY FAHAD KHAN TAREENOCTOBER 25, 20267 MIN READ
AKS FIELD NOTES← All articlesView projects ↗

When people picture a projection mapping show they picture light on a building. Behind that light sits a less glamorous system: mains power, cables and signal paths that run from a media server to every projector, often across a car park, a forecourt or a rooftop. Most of the failures we plan against on show night are not creative failures. They are a tripped breaker, a cable that is slightly too long, or a connector that works on the bench and drops out in the cold. This note explains how we think about power and signal for outdoor shows, what to ask your venue and supplier, and a table you can use to check a plan before it reaches site.

Start with the power budget

Power planning begins with a list of everything that will draw current: projectors, media servers, network switches, signal extenders, audio amplifiers, lighting, rigging controllers, monitors and crew lighting. Each device has a rated consumption on its data sheet. Add them up, then leave generous headroom, because projectors draw more at start-up than in steady operation and because a system running close to its limit has no room for the one extra device added on the day.

We separate the power question into two parts. The first is the total load. The second is how it is distributed: which circuits feed which equipment, and how long the feeder cables are. A supply that is large enough on paper can still fail if too many projectors share one circuit or if a long, thin extension cable causes a voltage drop at the far end. We ask a qualified electrician to confirm the supply and the distribution rather than relying on our own estimate, and we do not connect to a building supply that nobody on site can identify and isolate.

Using the venue supply is convenient but it comes with unknowns: shared circuits, supplies that serve lifts or air-conditioning, and breakers that someone else can switch off. A dedicated generator removes those unknowns and adds others: fuel, noise, placement and an operator who stays for the whole event. For large public shows a generator is often the practical choice because the equipment sites are away from any building.

Whichever you choose, the questions are the same. Who owns the supply, who may touch it, and who is on call if it trips? Where is the generator relative to the audience, given its noise and exhaust? Is there a second source, or at least a plan, for the critical equipment? Pakistan’s mains is nominally 230 V at 50 Hz, but equipment brought from other markets may expect a different range, so we check each device’s rated input before it is plugged in.

Protect the brains of the system

Not every device is equally important. A single projector going dark is visible but survivable. The media server or the show-control computer going down is the end of the show. We therefore put the servers, the network switch and the timing source on their own protected supply, usually through an uninterruptible power supply sized for the load, so that a brief dip or switchover does not reboot them. The projectors can then sit on a separate distribution. If the main supply drops, the system comes back in a known state instead of everything restarting at once and in a different order.

Surge protection and clean earthing matter here as well. Outdoor sites near generators, lighting rigs and long cable runs are electrically noisy, and a poor earth can show up as hum in the audio or as unexplained signal dropouts. We ask the electrician to check earthing before we connect anything sensitive.

Signal paths: how far can the picture travel?

The signal side is where outdoor shows most often surprise newcomers. Video leaves the media server over DisplayPort or HDMI, but those standards are designed for short runs. Over passive copper cable the maximum reliable length falls as the resolution and frame rate rise, and a cable that works at one resolution can fail at a higher one. A projector placed far from the server therefore needs another way to carry the signal.

The usual options are fibre-optic extenders, SDI over coaxial cable, or video over a network using specialised equipment. Fibre handles long distances and is immune to electrical interference, which suits runs near generators and lighting. SDI is a robust broadcast standard with locking connectors. Each approach has its own cable type and equipment, and the supplier’s data sheet should state the supported resolution, frame rate and maximum length. We follow those figures rather than rules of thumb, and we leave margin rather than running at the stated limit.

The related question of how many pixels you are sending is covered in our guide to canvas resolution, and the choice of server outputs is covered in choosing a media server. Together they decide how many signal runs you need and how heavy each one is.

Cable routing, protection and labelling

Cables on a public site are both a technical and a safety risk. People trip on them, vehicles run over them and rain gets into connectors. We route cables along edges and away from crowd routes where we can, cover those that must cross a walkway with proper protectors, and tape or clip them so that they cannot be pulled out. Connections outdoors are kept off the ground, covered from rain, and where possible fitted with locking connectors.

Labelling is the cheapest reliability measure we know. Each cable gets a label at both ends that says what it is and where it goes, and the labels match a drawing. When a projector goes dark at dusk, the person fixing it should not have to trace a cable by hand through a crowd of equipment. A clear signal diagram, kept with the crew and with the client, also makes it much easier to hand over or repeat the show later.

Redundancy: decide what you can afford to lose

We plan redundancy by asking what the show can survive. A spare projector in the same position covers a lamp or laser failure. A second media server mirroring the first covers a computer failure. A spare for each type of cable and extender covers the most common small faults. The right level depends on the audience, the venue and the cost of failure; a launch broadcast live has a different tolerance than a repeated seasonal show. Whatever is chosen, it should be written into the plan so that the client knows what is covered and what is not. Our note on show control and timecode covers the control side, including what happens when a source stops.

The final step is to run the entire system for as long as the show lasts, ideally longer, with every device powered the way it will be on the night. Short bench tests find broken parts; a long soak test finds overheating, intermittent connectors and tripped breakers. It belongs in the show-week plan, and our show-week schedule shows where it fits. We have delivered large public projections such as the ICC launch at Pakistan Monument in 2025 and shows at Parliament House in Islamabad, and on every one of them the plan for power and signal was written down before equipment was loaded.
