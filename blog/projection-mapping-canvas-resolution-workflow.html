Choosing canvas resolution for projection mapping

A practical workflow for balancing projector pixels, overlaps, content detail and render performance in large-scale projection mapping.

BY FAHAD KHAN TAREENOCTOBER 7, 20267 MIN READ
AKS FIELD NOTES← All articlesView projects ↗

A projection-mapping canvas should describe the pixels the audience can actually see, not simply the largest file a workstation can render. A canvas that is too small limits detail. One that is unnecessarily large slows previews, increases storage and makes last-minute delivery harder without improving the image on the building. The correct resolution comes from the complete projection system.

Start with the physical output layout

List every projector, its native output resolution, orientation and intended coverage. Draw the arrangement as a pixel map before opening the animation software. Include stacked or blended units as they will be addressed by the media server. This gives the content team a shared definition of the real output rather than an abstract image size.

Subtract overlap before counting usable pixels

Two side-by-side projectors do not always produce a canvas equal to the simple sum of their widths. Edge blending requires overlap, and those shared pixels appear only once on the building. Record the planned overlap for every seam and calculate the usable combined area. The final content canvas should reflect that visible space.

Relate pixels to architectural detail

Not every part of a façade needs equal pixel density. A detailed central entrance may carry faces or typography, while distant wings hold broad colour and movement. Evaluate which surfaces contain important information and how far the audience sits from them. This prevents spending render capacity on areas where additional detail cannot be perceived.

Separate the master canvas from delivery slices

Artists work more confidently when they see the full architectural composition. Build a master canvas representing the complete mapped surface, then define the slices or outputs required by the playback system. Keep naming and coordinates consistent so a revision in the master can be exported without rebuilding the technical layout.

Use a proxy workflow for speed

Large canvases can slow storyboarding, reviews and motion tests. Create lower-resolution proxies with the same aspect ratio and naming structure. Use them for timing, client reviews and early compositing, then switch to full resolution for detailed finishing and final renders. A reliable proxy workflow lets creative decisions happen quickly without changing the approved output geometry.

Test the smallest important detail

Before finishing the complete show, render a representative scene containing fine lines, type, texture and fast motion. Play it through the actual server and projection chain. Look for softening, scaling artifacts and details that disappear at the audience position. The test reveals whether the planned resolution is useful rather than merely large.

Respect codec and playback limits

A media server may support a large canvas while still struggling with a particular combination of dimensions, frame rate, codec and number of layers. Confirm delivery specifications early. Test a file with the final duration, compression and audio structure, and monitor playback rather than assuming a short loop represents the full programme.

Build safe areas around masks and seams

Important text, faces and small graphic elements should not sit directly across blend seams or at the edge of a mask. Small alignment changes can make those elements look uneven. Mark seam and mask guides in the creative template, and keep critical content inside stable architectural zones.

Keep the pipeline colour-consistent

Resolution decisions interact with colour and contrast. Scaling at different stages can soften edges, while inconsistent colour management can change the apparent clarity of dark detail. Agree on the working colour space, export settings and server interpretation. Test the complete pipeline on site before locking final grades.

Document one source of truth

Store the approved canvas dimensions, output slices, overlaps, frame rate and delivery settings in a simple technical sheet. Version the mapping template and final masters clearly. When the technical system or creative edit changes, update the same record. This prevents different teams from rendering against outdated assumptions.

The ideal canvas is not the biggest possible file. It is the most efficient digital description of the physical image: enough detail for the architecture and audience, organized for reliable playback, and practical for the artists who must build and revise it.
