Rigging and mounting projectors for outdoor shows

How to choose and check the structures that hold projectors outdoors: truss, scaffold, wind, vibration, fixings and a rigging checklist for planners.

BY FAHAD KHAN TAREENOCTOBER 26, 20266 MIN READ
AKS FIELD NOTES← All articlesView projects ↗

A projector is a precision optical instrument that we ask to sit still, outdoors, for hours, aimed at a target that may be a hundred metres away. If it moves by a fraction of a degree, the image on the building moves by a much larger distance, and the alignment we spent a night perfecting starts to drift. For that reason we treat rigging as part of the picture, not as a background detail. This note explains how we think about where projectors sit, what holds them, what can move them and how to check a rigging plan before show week.

Why a small movement becomes a visible one

Projection mapping is aligned by eye and by calibration against the architecture. The projector, its lens and the surface form a fixed geometry, and the warp applied in the media server assumes that geometry does not change. Because the beam travels a long way, a tiny rotation at the projector becomes a visible shift at the wall. A shift of a few pixels is enough to separate a mapped edge from the window or cornice it was meant to follow.

This is why a rigid, well-braced position matters more than a convenient one. A projector on a tall, thin stand in an open car park will move with every gust and every person who brushes past it. The same projector on a braced platform, clamped to a structure that does not sway, will hold its alignment through the show. The cost of the better position is usually small compared with the cost of re-aligning on the night.

Choose the structure for the job

There is no single correct support. The structure depends on the height needed, the ground, the number of projectors and the time available to build. We look at four common choices.

Tripods and stands. Suitable for a lightweight projector at a modest height on firm, level ground, and quick to set up. They are the first to suffer from wind and from people touching them, so we weigh them down, fence them and avoid them for heavy equipment on exposed sites.

Truss towers and ground-supported truss. Engineered truss with proper bases, bracing and ballast gives a rigid platform and lets us mount several projectors side by side. It needs a supplier who can supply load data and a person who can build it correctly, and it takes longer to assemble than a stand.

Scaffold platforms. A scaffold deck suits a position that needs both height and working space for the crew. It must be built and certified by a competent contractor, and projectors should be clamped to it rather than simply placed on it.

Rooftops and existing structures. Using a neighbouring roof or balcony can solve a throw-distance problem, but it brings access, permission and load questions that belong to the building owner. We never assume a roof can take the equipment; we ask the owner or their engineer.

Whichever you choose, the supplier’s documentation should state the safe working load, and the total weight of projectors, brackets, cables and any enclosure must stay well inside it. Our note on projector placement explains how throw distance and sightlines decide where the position should be; rigging is how you make that position stable.

What moves a rig

We plan against five causes of movement.

Wind. Enclosures, banners and the projectors themselves act as sails. Wind loading on a tall rig can be significant, so we ask the structure supplier for a wind limit and agree in advance the wind condition at which operations stop. Our guide to weather-proofing an outdoor show covers the wider weather plan.

Ground and settlement. Soft ground, paving over services and sloping surfaces can let a base sink or tilt as weight settles. We check the surface during the site survey and use spreader plates or level bases where the ground is uncertain.

Vibration. Generators, speakers, passing traffic and the projector’s own cooling fans all transmit vibration. A rig sharing a platform with a bass speaker array is a poor idea. We keep audio equipment off the projection structure and think about where the generator sits.

Heat. Projectors produce heat and need clear airflow. A cover that protects from rain but traps hot air will cause the projector to throttle or shut down. Enclosures must be designed for ventilation and for access to filters and vents, not just for weather protection.

People. Crowds, crew and vehicles are the most unpredictable cause. Barriers, clear exclusion zones and a named person responsible for the rig keep other people away from the equipment. This ties directly into the audience zones we plan so that the public can see the show without standing next to the projectors.

Mounting hardware and fixing the projector

The point where the projector meets the structure is where many problems start. We use purpose-made clamps and brackets rated for the weight, and we add a secondary safety attachment, such as a safety cable, so that a failed clamp does not become a falling projector. Bolts and quick-release pins should be locked or secured against loosening from vibration.

We also look for adjustment that can be locked. A mount that allows fine tilt, pan and roll saves time during alignment, but only if each axis can be tightened firmly once the image is in position. After alignment we mark the mount settings and, where practical, photograph them, so that a knocked projector can be put back to the recorded position instead of being realigned from scratch.

Lens choice and lens shift affect the rig too. A lens with the right throw ratio can bring the projector closer to the building and remove the need for a very tall structure. This is part of the same decision as projector brightness, because distance affects how bright the image appears on the surface.

Safety, access and people working at height

Rigging involves heavy equipment, height and public space, so safety is a first-order requirement. Competent people should build and inspect the structure, anyone working at height should have appropriate training and fall protection, and the area under a rig should be controlled while work is under way. We follow the requirements of the site, the structure supplier and the local authority, and we do not accept a position that nobody can inspect or reach safely. The approvals needed for public equipment sites are summarised in permissions for public projection mapping shows.

Cables leaving the rig need strain relief so that a pulled cable does not tug a projector off its mount. The routing and protection of those cables is covered in power and signal cabling for outdoor shows.

The last step is to build the rig in daylight, mount the projectors, run them for a full show length and watch whether the alignment holds. If the image creeps during that test, it will creep on the night. Our show-week schedule shows where this build and soak test sits relative to alignment and rehearsal. We have delivered building shows at locations such as Parliament House in Islamabad and Mohatta Palace in Karachi, and a stable, well-documented rig is part of every plan we write.
