How wall material and texture affect projection mapping

How stone, plaster, glass and textured walls change a projection mapping image: reflectance, relief, windows and a surface survey checklist for planners.

BY FAHAD KHAN TAREENOCTOBER 24, 20266 MIN READ
AKS FIELD NOTES← All articlesView projects ↗

Clients usually describe a projection mapping job by its building: the clock tower, the palace front, the new headquarters. What we ask about first is the wall itself. A projector only supplies light; the surface decides how much of it comes back to the audience, in what colour, and with what texture. Two facades of the same size can need very different projector power, content and expectations, simply because one is pale smooth plaster and the other is dark, rough or glazed. This note explains how common wall materials behave under projection, what to look for on a site visit and how to turn those observations into decisions before the quote and the content brief are fixed.

Reflectance: the surface is half the image

Every surface absorbs part of the light that lands on it and returns the rest. A pale, matte wall returns a large share and shows colour faithfully. A dark wall returns much less, so the same projector produces a dimmer image, and saturated colours lose their punch first. Because the audience judges the image by contrast, not by absolute brightness, a dark facade can look flat even under a strong projector. Our note on projector brightness explains why lumens alone cannot answer this, and our note on colour and contrast covers how to build a palette around it.

The practical lesson is to judge the wall at night, under the conditions of the show, not from a daytime photograph. Many surfaces that look white at noon are stained, weathered or tinted, and the difference only shows once light hits them. Where possible, the best check is a test projector on site, showing a plain colour field, before anyone commits to a promise.

Matte, glossy and the problem of reflections

Matte surfaces such as plaster, painted render, limestone and most stone scatter light in all directions, which is what you want: the image looks similar from many positions. Glossy and polished surfaces behave differently. Polished stone, glazed tile, painted metal and varnished panels reflect a portion of the light in one direction, like a mirror, so part of the beam bounces away from the audience and a hot spot can appear where the viewing angle matches the reflection. Sightlines matter more on these surfaces, which is one reason we study where the audience stands early.

Glass deserves its own mention. Clear glass lets most of the light pass through, so a glazed curtain wall mostly lights whatever is behind it, and it can also throw reflections back across the site. Frosted, fritted or heavily tinted glass can accept an image better, but the result is rarely as clean as a solid wall. Where a facade mixes glass and solid panels, we usually design the content to treat the windows as dark openings, and we confirm the choice with the client so nobody expects a continuous picture.

Texture, relief and joints

Rough, relief-rich surfaces catch light unevenly. Stone blocks, brick, carved ornament and deep mortar joints create small shadows that depend on the projector angle, so fine detail in the content can look noisy or lost. A hairline pattern that is crisp on a smooth wall may disappear into a brick texture from the audience position. In our experience, content for textured walls works best with bolder shapes, clear edges and slower detail, with the texture treated as part of the look rather than something to hide.

Depth also matters. Columns, arches, balconies, cornices and domes sit at different distances from the projector, which affects focus and the way the image falls across them. Projectors have a limited focus range, and a very deep facade can leave some parts slightly soft. Heritage fronts like the one in the photograph above, with domes, columns and arched openings, are rewarding for this reason: the architecture gives the content natural shapes to follow, but it also asks for careful alignment and for content designed to the building, not stretched over it. We cover that approach in our note on designing content for building facades.

Condition, colour and the things that change the look

A wall is rarely a clean, uniform surface. Look for:

  • Staining, water marks, soot or peeling paint, which show up as darker or uneven patches in the image.
  • Strong base colour, such as red brick, warm sandstone or dark cladding, which tints the projected colours. A white logo on a warm wall will look warm.
  • Surface fixtures such as lamps, signage, cameras, pipes and flags that cast shadows or catch the beam.
  • Vegetation, scaffolding or temporary hoardings that change the surface from one visit to the next.
  • Permanent lighting on the building, which raises the black level and reduces contrast unless it can be switched off.

On heritage sites there is an extra rule: the surface is not ours to alter, so the projection has to be planned around what is there. Any proposal to change the surface, including temporary coverings, needs the owner's written agreement, and we discuss it in our guide to heritage buildings.

Turning the survey into decisions

Once the surface is understood, three decisions follow. The first is projector power: a dark or heavily textured wall usually calls for more light, or for a smaller image area, than a pale smooth one. The second is the content brief: palette, line weight and level of detail should be set for the real surface, not a clean render of it. The third is expectation setting. We tell clients early which parts of a facade will look strongest and which will be modest, and we show a preview that includes the surface tone, which our previsualization workflow is designed to support. A client who has seen an honest preview is rarely disappointed on the night.

A difficult surface is not a reason to avoid a project; part of the craft is designing with the material. We have mapped heritage and public structures such as Noor Mahal, Rohtas Fort, Mohatta Palace in Karachi and Parliament House in Islamabad, and the first step on each was understanding what the surface would and would not do.
