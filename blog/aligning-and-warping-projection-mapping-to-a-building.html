Aligning and warping projection mapping to a building

How projection mapping is aligned to a building: warping, model-based and camera methods, audience position, blending, test patterns and drift checks.

BY FAHAD KHAN TAREENOCTOBER 29, 20266 MIN READ
AKS FIELD NOTES← All articlesView projects ↗

The moment a projection mapping show either convinces or disappoints is usually the moment the image lands on the building. If a window edge in the content sits even a little off the real window, the audience reads it as a projector on a wall rather than light that belongs to the architecture. Alignment is the work that closes that gap. This note explains how we think about it: what you are actually correcting, the main ways to do it, how to check the result, and what drifts afterwards.

What alignment actually corrects

A projector sends a flat, rectangular image from one point in space. A building is a set of surfaces at different depths, angles and heights, seen from a different point. Alignment, often called warping or mapping, is the process of distorting the image so that each part of it lands on the intended part of the structure when seen from the audience position.

Three things are being corrected together. The first is perspective, because the projector is rarely square to the wall. The second is the lens, since every lens bends the image a little. The third is the surface itself, where recesses, columns and cornices throw parts of the image forward or back. A good result needs all three handled, and a single corner-pin adjustment, the kind a basic projector offers, only deals with the first.

It also helps to be clear about the reference. The content is designed against a model or a drawing of the building, so alignment is really the act of making the real building match that reference. This is why the quality of the survey data matters so much, a point we make in our note on what to send a studio before a quote.

Manual, grid and camera-based methods

There are three broad approaches, and most real shows combine them.

Corner and grid warping. The operator drags the corners of the image onto known points, then adds a grid of control points across the surface and moves them to follow edges and features. It is slow on a large façade but gives the operator direct control, and it is the standard way to fine-tune any other method.

Model-based alignment. Here the building exists as a 3D model inside the media server or mapping software, and a virtual projector is positioned to match the real one. By matching several known points between the model and the physical surface, the software works out where the projector is and how its lens behaves, then renders the content to suit. This handles complex, multi-depth surfaces well, and it relies on a model that is accurate enough to trust. Our previsualization workflow builds the same model earlier in the project.

Camera-assisted alignment. A camera views the projection, and software projects test patterns and measures where they land. It can calibrate several projectors quickly and is particularly useful for blending. It depends on conditions, because strong ambient light, reflections and a camera that cannot see the whole surface all reduce its reliability. We treat it as a fast first pass that a person then checks and corrects by eye.

None of these removes the need for an experienced operator standing where the audience will stand. Software finds a good fit by measurement. The final judgement is visual.

Working from the audience position

A projection is only truly correct from one viewpoint, or from a narrow range of them, and the choice of that viewpoint should be deliberate. For a flat façade the difference across the crowd is small. For a building with deep recesses or a sculpted front, viewers off to the side will see content slide against the architecture, and the effect grows as the surface gets more three-dimensional.

We decide the reference position early, based on where most people will stand, and we check the image from the extremes of the viewing area as well as the centre. Our note on audience sightlines covers how to define that area. If the edges of the crowd see a visible error, the fix may lie in the content, such as softer edges or less dependence on exact depth cues, rather than in more warping.

Multiple projectors and the blend

Large buildings need several projectors, and each one must be aligned to the building and to its neighbours. The overlap between projectors is blended so that no seam shows, which requires both geometry and brightness to match across the join. A small geometric error in the overlap shows up as a doubled edge or a soft, ghosted line, so the order of work matters: align each projector to the structure first, then refine the overlaps. We cover the blend itself in edge blending for multi-projector shows.

Keep the projector settings fixed once alignment starts. Changing zoom, focus or lens shift after the warp has been built will move the image, and so will swapping a projector for a spare with a slightly different lens. If a unit has to be replaced, plan time to realign that section.

Checking the fit and keeping it

We check alignment with test content designed for the purpose rather than the show itself. Useful patterns include an outline of every window, edge and column; a fine grid; and a sequence that fills each surface in a flat colour so that spill onto neighbouring surfaces is easy to see. The aim is to find errors while there is still time to fix them, not to admire the finished artwork.

Then comes the part people forget. Alignment drifts. Projectors shift slightly as they warm and as their mounts settle, wind moves a lightly braced structure, and a knocked tripod or truss can undo hours of work. Good rigging reduces this, as described in our note on rigging and mounting projectors, but it cannot remove it. We re-check the fit on show day, once the equipment has settled and ideally in the same light conditions as the performance, and we leave time to correct small errors before the audience arrives. An alignment checklist

  • The reference viewing position is decided, and the edges of the audience area have been considered.
  • The model or drawings of the building have been checked against the real structure.
  • Projector zoom, focus and lens shift are locked before warping begins.
  • Each projector is aligned to the building first, then overlaps are refined.
  • Test patterns show windows, edges, columns and spill, not only the show content.
  • The fit is checked from the sides of the viewing area, not only the centre.
  • Time is reserved on show day to re-check and correct drift.
  • A spare projector is planned with the time needed to realign it.
