Audience sightlines for projection mapping shows

Where the audience stands decides how a projection mapping show reads. Learn about viewing zones, distance, angle, obstructions and a sightline checklist.

BY FAHAD KHAN TAREENOCTOBER 18, 20266 MIN READ
AKS FIELD NOTES← All articlesView projects ↗

A projection mapping show can be technically flawless and still disappoint if the audience is standing in the wrong place. The building does not move, the projectors do not move, but the people do, and what they see changes with every step they take. At AKS we treat the audience area as part of the design, not as something the event team sorts out the week before. This note explains how viewing position affects a show and what to settle with your producer before content is finished.

Start with the audience, not the projectors

Most planning conversations begin with the building and the equipment. We prefer to begin with a simple question: where will people stand, and how many of them will there be? A show for a few hundred invited guests in a courtyard is a different problem from a public night for a large crowd in an open square. The first allows a single, well-chosen viewpoint. The second means a wide range of positions, and the content has to survive all of them.

Ask the organiser for a site plan that shows entrances, barriers, stages, seating, food areas and any fixed obstacles. Mark the building and the projector positions on it, then draw the areas where people are expected to gather. That one drawing exposes most sightline problems before anyone has rented a projector.

Distance and angle

Two things define a viewing position: how far it is from the facade and how steeply the viewer looks up. Stand too close to a tall building and the upper floors are seen at a sharp angle. The image can feel compressed, and the viewer has to tilt their head back to take it in. Stand far away and the whole surface fits comfortably in the field of view, but fine detail and small text become harder to read.

As a working principle, a comfortable viewing area for a building-scale show usually starts at a distance that lets people see the full height of the content without craning, and extends back as far as the image stays clear. Where that boundary sits depends on the building, the brightness and the content, so we confirm it on site rather than quoting a rule. Our note on projector placement covers the throw side of this; the audience side is what we are dealing with here.

The sweet spot problem

Some content is flat graphics that read well from almost anywhere. Other content relies on perspective: shadows that fall as if a window were deeper than it is, objects that appear to step out of the wall, or geometry that seems to bend around a corner. These effects are built from a particular point of view, and they look most convincing from there. Move off to the side and the illusion weakens or distorts.

This is not a flaw, but it is a decision. If the content depends on a hero viewpoint, the audience area should be arranged around it, with the best position clearly designated for the main crowd or the guests who matter most. If the crowd will be spread widely, we lean on content that holds up from many angles: movement along architectural lines, light and colour washes, and graphics that follow the surface rather than imitate depth.

What gets in the way

Obstructions are the most common sightline failure, and many only appear on the night. Walk the audience area, at the height of a standing adult, and look for the following:

  • Trees, lamp posts, flagpoles and signage between the crowd and the building.
  • Parked vehicles, buses, stages, tents and food stalls placed after the survey.
  • Streetlights, shop signs and car headlights that wash out the image or sit in the audience’s eyeline.
  • Projector towers, scaffolding and cabling that block a segment of the crowd.
  • Gentle ground slopes or steps that leave the back rows looking over the heads of those in front.

Some can be removed or switched off by arrangement with the venue. Others have to be built into the design: if a tree will always cover part of the facade, the content should not place its key moment behind it.

Keeping people out of the light path

Audience planning also has to respect the projection. If projectors throw across an open area, anyone who walks into the beam casts a shadow on the building and can be dazzled by the projector lens. Barriers, marshals and clearly marked zones keep people out of the throw. Where it is practical, a raised or protected projector position keeps the beam above the crowd entirely. Crowd safety rules vary by venue and authority, so we work with the organiser and local requirements on capacity, exits and barriers rather than setting them ourselves.

Legibility: text, logos and detail

Sightlines decide what the audience can actually read. A logo that looks bold on a monitor can shrink to a smudge on a building when viewed from the back of a large crowd. Our approach is to size text and brand elements for the furthest position where people are expected to stand, to hold them on screen long enough to be read, and to keep fine detail for moments that do not depend on being understood. We cover this further in our note on designing content for building facades.

Sound travels differently from light

A good viewing position is no use if the sound is wrong there. Audio arrives later than light over distance, and the audience at the back hears the show behind what they see unless the system is delayed to compensate. We plan speaker positions and timing alongside the viewing area, as described in our note on sound for outdoor projection mapping shows.

A sightline checklist

QuestionWhat to confirm
Where will people stand?A site plan with marked audience zones and expected numbers
Is there a best viewpoint?A designated position if the content uses perspective effects
Can everyone see the full surface?A walk of front, middle and back positions at standing eye height
What blocks the view?Trees, vehicles, stages, stalls and equipment, checked on the night
What competes with the image?Street lighting, signage and headlights, with a plan to dim or shield
Is the beam path clear?Barriers or marshals keeping people out of the projector throw
Can the back rows read the content?Text and logos sized for the furthest viewing position
Does the sound match the view?Speaker coverage and delay checked across the audience area

Test it at night, with people

Daytime visits tell you the geometry. They do not tell you how the street looks at nine in the evening. We prefer to run a test projection during rehearsal, then walk the audience area looking at the building from the places real guests will stand. Taking a few photographs from the front, middle and back positions is useful evidence for the whole team, and it often prompts small but valuable changes: moving a barrier, dimming a light, or re-timing a key moment.
