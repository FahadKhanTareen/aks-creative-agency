Interactive projection mapping: sensors, latency, safety

How interactive projection mapping works: choosing sensors, controlling latency, designing for idle and crowded states, safety, and a planning checklist.

BY FAHAD KHAN TAREENNOVEMBER 4, 20267 MIN READ
AKS FIELD NOTES← All articlesView projects ↗

Interactive projection mapping lets the audience change what happens on the surface: a wave of colour follows a hand, a building facade reacts to a crowd's movement, or a floor lights up under footsteps. It is a strong idea for launches and festivals, and it is also one of the easiest ways to turn a reliable show into a fragile one. A linear show can be rehearsed until it is identical every night. An interactive one depends on sensors, software and unpredictable people. At AKS we treat interactivity as a separate layer on top of a solid linear show, and this note explains how we think about sensors, latency, content design and safety when a client asks for it.

Decide what interaction is actually for

Start with the purpose. Interaction can make visitors feel part of a brand moment, give a dwell-time installation a reason to exist, or let a crowd trigger a finale. It does not make a show better by default. On a large building seen by thousands of people from a distance, only a few can be tracked, and everyone else watches the result without understanding the cause. Interaction works best when the effect is readable from where people stand and when the link between action and reaction is obvious within a second or two.

It helps to separate three levels. In the first, a single operator or presenter triggers cues live, which is closer to show control than to audience interaction. In the second, a small group of selected participants, such as guests on a stage, drive the visuals. In the third, anyone in a defined zone can take part. Each level adds risk and cost, so choose the lowest one that meets the goal.

Choosing a sensing method

Several technologies can tell a media server where people are or what they are doing. Each has strengths and weaknesses outdoors at night, which is where most of our work happens.

  • Depth cameras read the shape and position of bodies, but many consumer models are designed for indoor use and can be disturbed by strong sunlight or other infrared sources.
  • Standard video cameras with software tracking are flexible and can cover wider areas, though they depend on light levels and on the camera not being fooled by the projected image itself.
  • Infrared or laser-scanning sensors detect objects crossing a plane, which suits floors and walls at close range.
  • Pressure or touch surfaces are dependable for floors and tables but are limited to the area they cover.
  • Phones and handheld controllers move the sensing problem to the audience's devices, which depends on network quality and on people being willing to join.
  • Microphones and other sensors can drive visuals from loudness or movement without tracking any individual.

The projector itself is a complication. A camera that watches an area lit by bright projected content may see the content instead of the people. Designers usually deal with this by using infrared-based sensing, masking the projected area from the tracker, or placing the sensor so that it views the audience rather than the surface. Whichever method is chosen, test it on site under the real lighting, not in the studio.

Latency decides whether it feels interactive

Latency is the delay between an action and the visible response. It accumulates along the whole chain: the sensor captures, the software processes, the media server renders, and the projector displays. Each step adds a little, and the total is what the participant feels. If a person waves a hand and the effect appears noticeably later, the link is broken and the interaction feels like a trick played on them rather than something they control.

There is no single number that works for every case, and published figures vary with equipment, so measure rather than assume. A practical test is to film the participant and the surface together and count the frames between movement and response. Ways to keep latency down include:

  • using a direct connection between sensor and computer instead of passing data through several devices;
  • keeping the interactive layer light so the media server is not struggling to render it;
  • turning off projector image processing modes that add delay, where the projector allows it;
  • designing effects that tolerate a small delay, such as trails and ripples that fade, rather than effects that must line up exactly with a body.

Content design is the cheapest tool here. A soft, flowing response hides small delays well, while a hard-edged shape that has to sit exactly on a person's hand exposes every one of them. For the pixel and playback side of the chain, our notes on choosing a media server and on codecs and frame rates are relevant.

Design content that fails gracefully

Real audiences do unexpected things. Nobody may step into the zone, twenty people may step in at once, or someone may cover a sensor with a bag. Content should look good in every one of those states. We build an interactive show in layers: a strong linear base layer that plays regardless of input, and an interactive layer that adds to it. If the sensing fails, the base layer carries on and most viewers will not notice that anything is missing.

Plan for the idle state, the single-user state and the crowded state separately. In the idle state the surface should attract attention rather than sit dark. With one user, the response should be clear and personal. With a crowd, the system should blend inputs or cap the number of tracked points so that the image does not turn to noise. Set limits for how strong or fast any response can become, in line with the guidance in our note on flicker and strobe safety, because a crowd can drive the visuals harder than any designer would.

Safety and crowd behaviour

Interaction changes how people move. They look at the surface, step backwards to see it, and sometimes run towards an effect. Position sensors and zones so that taking part never requires entering a roadway, a loading area, a drop or the space behind a barrier. Keep cables, stands and sensors out of walking lines, and secure them against knocks. Marked zones and clear signage help people understand where to stand, and staff or volunteers should be briefed on what to do if a crowd forms around a sensor.

Think also about who can take part. Effects that rely on reaching, jumping or standing in a particular spot may exclude some visitors, so offer more than one way to trigger a response. Where cameras track people, check the rules that apply to the venue about recording the public, and avoid storing images when only positions are needed. The approvals involved are described in our note on permissions for public projection mapping shows.

Rehearsal, operation and backup

An interactive layer needs its own rehearsal time, and it needs people who are not part of the crew. Test with strangers at the venue if you can, since they will not behave as the team does. Rehearse the failure cases deliberately: unplug the sensor, cover it, and shut the software down, then confirm the base show continues and that an operator can restart the interactive layer without stopping playback.

Give the operator a clear way to disable interaction quickly, and an agreed rule about when to do so. If weather, crowd density or a technical fault makes the interaction unreliable, a clean fallback to the linear show is better than a broken effect. The same thinking applies to the whole system, as we describe in our note on backup and redundancy, and to show control and cueing, covered in our note on timecode and show control.

An interactive projection checklist

  • The purpose of the interaction is written down, and a simpler level has been considered.
  • The sensing method is tested on site, at night, under the real projected content.
  • Latency has been measured end to end and the content is designed to tolerate it.
  • A linear base layer plays whether or not anyone interacts.
  • Idle, single-user and crowded states have all been designed and tested.
  • Limits on speed, brightness and flashing apply to every interactive response.
  • Sensors, cables and zones are clear of roads, drops and walking lines.
  • Rules on tracking and recording the public have been checked for the venue.
  • Failure cases have been rehearsed, and the operator can disable interaction quickly.

Interactive projection can be memorable when it is planned as part of the production rather than added at the end.
