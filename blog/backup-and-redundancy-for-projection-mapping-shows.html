Backup and redundancy for projection mapping shows

What to duplicate in a projection mapping show: media servers, projectors, power, content and procedure, with a table and checklist for planners.

BY FAHAD KHAN TAREENOCTOBER 27, 20267 MIN READ
AKS FIELD NOTES← All articlesView projects ↗

Every projection mapping show has one night, one audience and a long chain of equipment that all has to work at the same moment. Most shows run without a fault. The ones that do fail usually fail in a small, ordinary place: a loose cable, a tripped breaker, a frozen playback machine, an operator who is unsure which button restarts the show. Redundancy is how we decide, before show week, which of those failures the audience will never see. This note explains how we think about backup planning, what is worth duplicating, and how to review a plan without turning it into an unaffordable one.

Start with what can fail and what it costs

We begin by drawing the show as a chain: content on storage, a media server, signal to the projectors, the projectors themselves, power under all of it, and the control link to sound and lighting. For each link we ask two questions. How likely is it to fail, and what does the audience see if it does? A single dead projector in a ten-projector array may leave a dark patch. A dead media server may leave the whole building dark. Those two failures do not deserve the same budget.

This is a judgement, not a formula. A short list of single points of failure, ranked by consequence, is more useful than a long list of everything that could theoretically go wrong. It also keeps the conversation with the client honest: backup is a purchase, and it should be spent where it protects the show most. Our note on what drives the cost of a projection mapping show explains where this sits in the wider budget.

Playback: the media server and the content

The media server is the most consequential single point in the chain, because everything the audience sees passes through it. The usual approach is a second machine with the same software, the same content and the same project, ready to take over. How ready depends on the show and the budget.

Cold spare. A second machine, configured and tested, sitting beside the first but not running. If the main machine fails, the crew switches signal cables and starts the show again. It costs the least and is the slowest to recover.

Hot spare. A second machine running the same show, in step with the first, with its outputs ready to be switched in. Recovery is faster, and the audience may see only a brief change. It needs a way to keep the two machines aligned, usually timecode or a shared start signal, and a switching method for the video outputs.

Whichever you choose, the content must exist in more than one place. We keep a copy on the playback machine’s own drive, a copy on a second machine and an offline copy on a separate drive that leaves the site with a named person. Content should be copied and then checked by playing it, not assumed good because the copy finished. The media server selection note lists what to look for in outputs, codecs and redundancy features when you compare products.

Projectors and the display chain

Projectors fail more gently than servers, but they fail. Lamps and laser sources age, filters clog, and heat can cause a projector to reduce its output or shut down. Our first protection is simple: a spare projector of the same model and lens on site, with a known mount position and a recorded set of alignment settings. If you have a spare, a failure becomes a short swap, not a cancelled section of the show.

Where the image is shared between overlapping projectors, an outage has a particular effect. If one projector in a blended pair stops, the other still lights its half of the overlap but at reduced brightness, and the blend region becomes visible. We plan so that a loss can be recognised quickly and, if it cannot be fixed during the show, the content can fall back to a version that tolerates a missing section. The mechanics of overlaps are explained in edge blending for multi-projector projection mapping.

The cable between server and projector is a link people forget. A video connection that works on a test bench can behave differently over a long outdoor run, so we test the real run, carry spare cables of every type and length used, and label both ends clearly so that a stressed crew member can identify the right one quickly.

Power: the quiet single point of failure

When a show goes dark without warning, power is often the reason. A generator can run out of fuel, trip on overload or stall, and a shared circuit can fail under a load nobody counted. We separate the critical equipment from everything else, keep media servers and network equipment on a protected supply where we can, and plan for the show to survive a short interruption in mains or generator output without the playback machines rebooting.

The same thinking applies to the generator itself. A second unit, or at least a plan for how fuel is monitored and topped up, belongs in the written plan. Our note on power and signal cabling for outdoor projection mapping goes into circuits, protection and cable routing in more detail. The principle here is that a duplicated server is of no use if both machines are fed from a single socket.

People and procedure are part of the backup

Equipment redundancy is wasted if nobody knows how to use it. A written fault procedure is as valuable as a spare machine. For each likely failure we write down who notices it, who decides, what the recovery steps are and how long the audience should wait before a message or a pause is used. During rehearsal we rehearse a failure on purpose: we pull a cable or stop the primary machine and time how long the recovery takes. The first time a team switches to backup should never be in front of the public.

Communication matters as much as hardware. A single named show caller, a reliable intercom channel between the control position, the projector crew and the audio operator, and a shared understanding of the words that mean stop, hold and restart all reduce the time between a fault and a decision. This connects closely to the show-control setup described in show control and timecode for projection mapping, because a clean restart depends on a reliable cue structure.

We also plan the audience-facing fallback. A hold frame, a simple slow loop or a calm graphic that can play while a fault is fixed is far better than a black wall or a visible error screen. It should look intentional, match the colour and tone of the show and be ready to trigger at any point. Our field note on designing a show for the unexpected describes the same idea from the creative side.

What to duplicate and what to accept

No plan removes all risk, and trying to is how budgets become unreasonable. The table below shows how we usually think about each link in the chain. The right answer for a particular show depends on its scale, its audience and the consequence of a gap.

LinkTypical protectionAccept if
Content filesCopies on two machines and an offline drive, all test-playedNever accept a single copy
Media serverCold or hot spare with identical projectThe show is short and a restart is acceptable
ProjectorsSpare unit with lens and recorded settingsA lost section can be hidden by content design
CablesSpares of every type and length, labelledNever accept missing spares
PowerSeparate protected supply for critical gear, fuel planBrief interruptions are survivable by design
Control and audioBackup trigger method and manual startThe show can run unsynced for a short while
PeopleWritten fault procedure, rehearsed recoveryNever accept an untested procedure

A short backup checklist

Before show day, we confirm the following with the client and the crew:

  • A ranked list of single points of failure exists and each has a decision: duplicate, spare or accept.
  • Content is stored in at least three places and each copy has been played back successfully.
  • A spare media server and a spare projector are on site and have been tested, not just packed.
  • Critical equipment sits on its own protected power, and the generator has a fuel and monitoring plan.
  • A fault procedure is written, with named roles, and has been practised at least once during rehearsal.
  • A hold frame or fallback loop is loaded and can be triggered at any time.

We have delivered building shows at places such as Parliament House in Islamabad and Rohtas Fort, and the habit is the same on each site: decide in advance what we will do when something goes wrong, so that on the night the answer is already a rehearsed procedure.
