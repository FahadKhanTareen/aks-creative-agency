Show control and timecode for projection mapping

How timecode, media server playlists, cue triggers and backup plans keep a projection mapping show locked to sound, lighting and effects on the night.

BY FAHAD KHAN TAREENOCTOBER 14, 20265 MIN READ
AKS FIELD NOTES← All articlesView projects ↗

A projection mapping show is rarely just pictures. On the night, the same moment may need to trigger a projector scene, a lighting change, a pyrotechnic cue, a music swell and a speaker announcement. If those systems drift apart by even a fraction of a second, the audience feels it before they can explain it. Show control is the discipline that keeps everything locked together, and it deserves planning as early as the creative.

What timecode actually does

Timecode is a shared clock expressed as hours, minutes, seconds and frames. When the media server, audio playback and lighting console all read the same clock, each department can place its cues at exact positions in the show instead of starting manually and hoping to stay in step. At AKS we treat the timeline as the single source of truth: if a moment is at a given timecode in the content, every other system refers to that same moment.

Timecode comes in several forms. SMPTE linear timecode is an audio signal that can travel over an ordinary audio cable. MIDI timecode carries the same information as MIDI messages, and many lighting and media tools can also receive timecode over a network. Which one you use depends on the equipment each department already owns. What matters more than the format is that everyone agrees on one master and one frame rate before content is built.

Choose one master, and decide who follows

The most common mistake is having two systems that both believe they are in charge. Decide in advance which device generates the clock. In many shows this is the audio playback system, because sound is the element the audience is least able to forgive when it stutters, and the media server and lighting console chase it. In others, the media server is the master and sends timecode out. Either approach works if it is written down and rehearsed.

Frame rate needs the same discipline. The timecode frame rate, the content frame rate and the media server project settings should be consistent. Mixing rates can produce drift or an off-by-a-few-frames offset that only appears late in a long programme. Settle it in the technical brief, before artists begin rendering.

Cue lists, playlists and what they trigger

Not every show runs as one unbroken file. A typical programme may have a pre-show loop, a main show and a post-show loop, plus separate clips for transitions. A media server playlist or cue list organizes these, and an operator or the show controller steps through them. We prefer to keep the main show as one continuous timeline wherever possible, because fewer handoffs mean fewer places for something to go wrong.

Outside the timeline, some triggers still need a human decision: a dignitary arriving late, a speech that overruns, a weather hold. Plan for a manual go button that starts the timeline from a known clean state, and a defined way to return to the loop. A show that can only run perfectly from the start is fragile.

Network, trigger protocols and keeping it simple

Modern shows pass many signals over a network: timecode, cue triggers, status and sometimes lighting data. Use a dedicated, well-organised network for show control rather than sharing the venue's general Wi-Fi. Fix IP addresses, label cables and switches, and keep a diagram of what talks to what. Wireless links are convenient for a tablet remote, but the timecode and trigger path for the show itself should be wired wherever practical.

Common triggering options include OSC, MIDI, DMX-based control and simple contact closures, and the right choice depends on what your media server and the other departments support. A simple, well-understood trigger is usually more dependable than a clever one. Every extra protocol conversion is another thing to test, and another thing to explain to a replacement operator at short notice.

Latency, offsets and the real world

Different equipment takes different amounts of time to turn a signal into light. A projector and its processing chain adds latency, an LED wall adds its own, and sound takes noticeable time to travel across a large site. A crowd standing far from the speakers hears the audio later than the picture, which matters for a building-scale outdoor show. We measure and compensate during rehearsal, in the place where the audience will stand, rather than trusting numbers on a datasheet.

Keep an offset sheet. If audio needs to be advanced or delayed against the picture, record the value, who set it and why. This prevents a well-meaning change on show day from undoing a correction made carefully during the technical rehearsal.

Redundancy and the backup plan

Ask what happens if the primary media server fails. Options range from a second server running in parallel with a manual switchover to a simpler fallback loop that keeps the building looking intentional. The right level depends on the event: a launch with an invited audience and broadcast coverage justifies more redundancy than a small community screening. Whatever you choose, rehearse the switchover so the operator has done it before the night it counts.

Also protect against the quiet failures. A timecode source that stops should not leave the show frozen without anyone noticing, so give the operator a clear status display and an agreed call-out. Keep spare cables, a spare power adapter for the controller and a copy of the show files on a second drive at the control position.

A show control checklist

  • One named timecode master, written in the technical brief.
  • Frame rate agreed across content, media server, audio and lighting.
  • Dedicated show-control network with fixed addresses and a simple diagram.
  • Main show built as one continuous timeline where possible.
  • Manual start, hold and return-to-loop procedures defined and rehearsed.
  • Audio and picture offsets measured at the audience position and recorded.
  • Backup server or fallback loop tested, including the switchover.
  • Show files duplicated at the control position, with spare cables and power.
  • Operator briefed, with a written cue sheet and a clear call-out routine.

Rehearse the whole chain

Show control problems rarely appear in isolation. They appear when audio, lighting, effects and projection run together for the first time. Reserve technical rehearsal time to run the complete show from the real control position, at least once without stopping. Treat any unexplained jump, freeze or drift as a fault to be solved, not a quirk to be worked around.

Good show control is invisible. The audience just sees a building that responds to music and light as though it were alive.
