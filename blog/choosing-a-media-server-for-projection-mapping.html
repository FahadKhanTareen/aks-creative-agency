Choosing a media server for projection mapping

How to choose a media server for projection mapping: outputs, codecs, warping and blending, timecode, redundancy and a checklist to compare options.

BY FAHAD KHAN TAREENOCTOBER 19, 20267 MIN READ
AKS FIELD NOTES← All articlesView projects ↗

Ask five show crews what a media server is and you will get five slightly different answers, because the term covers everything from a single workstation running playback software to a rack of synchronised machines driving dozens of outputs. For a client or production manager, the practical question is simpler: which system will play our content reliably, on this building, on the night? At AKS we choose the server after we understand the surface, the content and the show control plan, never before. This note explains what a media server does in a projection mapping show and how to compare the options without getting lost in brand names.

What a media server actually does

A media server plays back video and generative content, maps it onto one or more outputs, and keeps that playback in step with the rest of the show. In a projection mapping job it usually has four duties. It decodes or renders the content at the full canvas resolution. It splits that canvas into slices, one per projector. It warps and blends those slices so they land correctly on the architecture and merge seamlessly where they overlap. And it follows an external clock or trigger so the picture stays locked to sound, lighting and effects.

Some systems are dedicated media servers with their own control software. Others are general-purpose playback or real-time engines running on a high-specification PC. Both can deliver a good show. What matters is that the system can do all four duties at the scale of your project, and that your crew knows it well enough to fix a problem quickly in the dark.

Start from the canvas, not the catalogue

The first filter is the size of the canvas. Our note on choosing canvas resolution explains how projector pixels, overlaps and content detail add up. Once you know the total pixel count and the frame rate, you can ask the real question: can one machine play it, or do we need several?

A modest single-facade show may fit on one machine with a few outputs. A wide building, a large number of projectors or very high resolution content may call for several machines running in sync, with one acting as the lead. Splitting the load is routine, but it adds synchronisation, networking and spares to the plan, and your quote should show that. If a supplier says one machine will handle everything, ask for the numbers behind it: the canvas size, the codec, the frame rate and the number of outputs.

Codecs, storage and playback headroom

Large canvases are demanding to play back. Heavily compressed video is easy to store but can strain the processor when decoded at very high resolution. Codecs designed for fast, frame-accurate playback are lighter to decode but produce much larger files. Neither is universally better; the right choice depends on the server, the storage and the resolution.

We care about three practical points. First, the files should be tested on the actual show machine, not only on the editing workstation, because a clip that plays smoothly on one system can stutter on another. Second, storage must be fast enough to stream the files without dropped frames, so we look at drive type and read speed rather than just capacity. Third, we keep headroom. A system that runs close to its limit in the workshop will not have room to spare when a last-minute change arrives or the machine runs hot outdoors.

Warping, blending and calibration tools

Mapping is not just playback. The server has to place the image on the surface precisely. Look for the ability to warp each output with a mesh, mask areas that should stay dark, blend overlapping projectors and adjust colour and brightness per output. These tools are where much of the on-site time goes, so how quickly and cleanly they work matters as much as raw performance.

It is also worth asking how the system stores alignment. When a projector is knocked or a lens is changed, can the team correct one output without redoing everything? Can a good calibration be saved and recalled? Projection mapping on a heritage site such as Rohtas Fort or Noor Mahal rewards a workflow that lets the crew make small, safe corrections, because the surface is irregular and time on site is limited.

Show control and sync

The server rarely works alone. It has to follow timecode or accept cues from a show controller, and it has to do so predictably. Our note on show control and timecode covers the options in detail. For server selection, confirm that the system can chase an external timecode source, accept network or serial triggers from your control desk, and recover cleanly if the signal drops and returns. Ask to see this tested, not described.

If several machines share a canvas, they also need to agree with each other frame by frame. Ask the supplier how that synchronisation is achieved, what hardware it requires and what happens to the picture if one machine falls behind.

Redundancy and the plan for failure

Every machine fails eventually; the question is what the audience sees when it does. A sensible plan for a public show decides this in advance. Depending on the scale and the stakes, that can mean a spare machine on the shelf, a mirrored backup running in parallel that can take over, or a simple fallback such as a held frame or a clean black while the crew recovers. A client attending a high-profile launch should know which of these is in the budget and which risk is being accepted.

Redundancy also includes the small things: spare power supplies, spare graphics outputs, known-good cables, a tested copy of the show files on a second drive and a documented way to restart the show from the start or from a chosen cue. We would rather rehearse a failure once on a quiet afternoon than meet it for the first time in front of a crowd. Our note on designing for the unexpected describes the creative side of the same idea.

Operators matter more than logos

The most capable server in the hands of an unfamiliar operator is a risk. We favour systems our team knows well, with a clear support route, over a newer product with a longer feature list. If you are hiring a crew separately from the content studio, make sure the operator has used the chosen system on a similar job and will attend the rehearsal, not only the show.

Also consider licensing and hardware lead times. Some systems are tied to licence keys or specific hardware, and a failed machine may not be replaced in an afternoon. Ask who holds the licences and how a replacement would be activated on site.

A media server checklist

QuestionWhat to confirm
Can it play the canvas?Total resolution, frame rate and number of outputs, tested on the show machine
Is the codec suitable?Smooth playback at full resolution with headroom, from fast storage
Are the mapping tools adequate?Per-output warping, masking, blending and colour adjustment
Can alignment be saved?Stored calibrations and a way to correct one output alone
Does it follow the show clock?Timecode or trigger input, tested with recovery after a dropout
Do multiple machines stay in sync?A stated method and a known behaviour if one machine lags
What happens if it fails?Spare or backup machine, fallback picture and a restart procedure
Who runs it?An operator experienced with this system, present at rehearsal

Questions to put to any supplier

When comparing quotes, we suggest asking the same short list of questions of every supplier. What is the full canvas size and frame rate you are planning for? How many machines and outputs will be used? What is the backup plan? Who will operate the system on show night? Has the content been tested on the actual playback machine? Clear answers usually mean a team that has done the work before; vague ones are a reason to look more closely, whatever the equipment list says.
