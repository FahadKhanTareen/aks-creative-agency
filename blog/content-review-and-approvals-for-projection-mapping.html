Content review and approvals for projection mapping

How to review and approve projection mapping content: stages, who signs off, what feedback helps, a revision limit and a checklist to protect your date.

BY FAHAD KHAN TAREENNOVEMBER 1, 20267 MIN READ
AKS FIELD NOTES← All articlesView projects ↗

Most projection mapping schedules do not slip because of projectors or rigging. They slip because the content is still changing a few days before the show. A logo is replaced, a sentence is reworded, a new stakeholder appears and asks for a different ending. On a building-scale canvas every one of those changes costs render time, and render time is the one resource that cannot be borrowed from show week. At AKS we have learned to treat review and approval as a designed part of the production rather than something that happens by good intention. This note sets out how we structure it, so that clients and production managers know what to expect and what we will ask of them.

Why review is harder for mapped content

On a normal video, a reviewer watches a file on a laptop and gives notes. Projection mapping content is different in three ways. First, the picture is a very wide canvas that is made to fit a building, so on a flat screen it can look distorted or confusing. Second, the final look depends on the surface, the ambient light and the viewing position, none of which exist on a laptop. Third, the animation is cut to music, so a small change to timing can ripple through every scene after it.

That means a reviewer who only sees a flat preview may approve something that does not work on the wall, or reject something that would have looked right. Good review practice gives people a preview that is as close to the real condition as the stage allows, and it is honest about what the preview cannot show. Our previsualization workflow is the foundation for this, because a digital model of the building lets a reviewer see content wrapped onto the architecture before a single projector is on site.

Agree the stages before the work starts

We divide content into stages and ask for a sign-off at the end of each one. The exact names vary by project, but the logic is constant: decisions are made from broad to narrow, and once a stage is approved it is locked unless the client accepts the consequence in time and cost.

  • Concept and story. The idea, the sequence of scenes and the message. Approval here fixes what the show is about.
  • Storyboard and timing. Frames for each scene against the soundtrack. Approval here fixes the order and the length. Our guide to storyboarding a projection show covers this in detail.
  • Style frames. A few finished frames that set colour, texture and typography on the building model.
  • Rough animation. Motion and rhythm with simple lighting and placeholder detail, previewed on the model.
  • Full render review. Final quality for each scene, checked at a size and viewing distance that matches the real audience.
  • On-site check. A short test on the real surface, where only fine adjustments to colour, brightness and position should remain.

The earlier a change is requested, the cheaper it is. A different ending at the storyboard stage costs a conversation. The same change after the full render may mean re-animating and re-rendering several scenes. We say this plainly at the start so the stages are understood as a protection for the client as much as for the studio.

Name one decision maker

The single most useful thing a client can do is name one person who can say yes. Projection mapping often involves a brand team, an events agency, a building owner and sometimes a government or institutional partner, and each may have a view. If notes arrive separately from four people and contradict each other, the studio cannot resolve them and the schedule stops.

We ask for a single point of contact who collects feedback, settles disagreements internally and sends one consolidated list. Where a third party such as a building owner has the right to approve the use of its facade or the tone of the content, we ask for that approval route to be named early, and we show them the relevant stage rather than the final render. This sits alongside the approvals described in our note on permissions for public projection mapping shows, and it is wise to ask those parties whether they want to see content at all, and when.

Give feedback that can be acted on

Feedback is most useful when it describes a problem and refers to a precise place. We ask reviewers to give a time in the animation, the scene name and what they see, rather than a general reaction. Compare these two notes:

  • "It feels a bit flat." This is a feeling, and several different fixes could answer it.
  • "At the second minute, the transition into the courtyard scene is too slow for the music, and the logo is hard to read." This names the place, the issue and the reason.

It also helps to separate three kinds of note. Corrections are errors, such as a wrong name, a wrong date or a misplaced logo, and should always be fixed. Preferences are matters of taste, and they can be weighed against time. Changes of direction are new ideas that alter the brief, and they should be priced and scheduled as such. Many disputes disappear once everyone agrees which of the three a note belongs to.

Control the number of revision rounds

An open-ended review has no end. We agree a set number of revision rounds at each stage, usually one or two, and we write it into the plan. A round means one consolidated set of notes and one revised version, not a continuous flow of small messages. Notes that arrive after a round has closed move into the next round or are logged as additional work.

Corrections to factual errors are usually kept outside the count, because nobody benefits from a misspelt name on a wall. Everything else needs a rule, and the rule should be visible to the whole client team. We find that a shared review calendar, with dates when feedback is due and dates when new versions will be sent, does more to keep a project on time than any individual reminder. Content that must also meet technical delivery rules, such as the file formats in our note on video codecs and frame rates, should be reviewed against those rules before the final round, not after it.

Review on the right screen, in the right light

Reviewing a night show on a bright office monitor leads to wrong decisions. Dark scenes look clear on a screen but vanish on a wall that is lit by street lighting, and saturated colours behave differently on stone and plaster. We recommend that reviewers watch the previews in a dimmed room, on a screen that is large enough to see detail, and that they are told which scenes are most sensitive to the real surface. Our notes on colour and contrast and wall material explain why those differences exist.

The on-site check is the one point where the whole team sees the truth. Because projection mapping content is judged against a physical surface, we plan time for this check in the show-week schedule and keep the permitted changes small: brightness, contrast, colour balance, position and the timing of a cue. Anything bigger should be flagged as a risk to the show, because it needs re-rendering and a new test.

A content approval checklist

  • One named decision maker can approve or reject on behalf of the client.
  • Third-party approvers, such as a building owner, are identified, with the stage they will see.
  • Each stage has a written sign-off and a date.
  • The number of revision rounds is agreed and shared with the whole client team.
  • Feedback is sent as one consolidated list with scene names and times.
  • Notes are labelled as corrections, preferences or changes of direction.
  • Reviewers watch previews that show the content on the building model, in a dimmed room.
  • Logos, names, dates and legal wording are checked at the storyboard stage, not the final render.
  • Time for an on-site check is in the schedule, with the permitted adjustments listed.
  • A content lock date is set before show week, and later changes are treated as risks.

A clear approval process does not limit creativity. It protects the time the creative team needs to make the final render excellent.
