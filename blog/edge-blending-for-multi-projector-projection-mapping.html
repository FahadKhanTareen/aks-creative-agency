Edge blending for multi-projector projection mapping

How edge blending hides projector seams: overlap size, pixel cost, black level, seam placement and projector matching, plus a checklist for large shows.

BY FAHAD KHAN TAREENOCTOBER 22, 20267 MIN READ
AKS FIELD NOTES← All articlesView projects ↗

Any building wider than a single projector can cover needs two or more projectors working as one image. The place where their beams meet is the seam, and a poorly handled seam is the first thing a trained eye finds: a bright vertical stripe, a darker band, or a faint doubling of fine lines. Edge blending is the technique that makes that seam disappear. It sounds like a software setting, but whether it works depends on decisions made much earlier, from lens choice to content design. At AKS we treat the overlap zones as part of the design, not as a problem to fix on site. This note explains how blending works, what it costs in pixels and contrast, and how to plan for it.

What edge blending actually does

When two projectors overlap, the shared area receives light from both, so it is roughly twice as bright as the area lit by one. Blending corrects this by fading each projector's output across the overlap, so that the contribution from one falls as the other rises and the sum stays level with the rest of the image. The fade is applied by the media server or the projector, and it is shaped by a curve rather than a straight line, because the response of a projector to a pixel value is not linear. A blend that ignores this will leave the overlap visibly brighter or darker than its surroundings, even if the maths looks right on paper.

Blending only works if the two images are also geometrically aligned. If the same architectural edge lands in slightly different positions from each projector, the overlap shows a soft double image no matter how well the brightness is matched. Warping and blending are therefore done together: first align the projected pixels to the surface and to each other, then apply the fade.

How much overlap you need

There is no single correct figure, but overlaps commonly fall somewhere around 10 to 20 percent of the projector width. A wider overlap gives a longer, gentler fade that hides small errors in alignment and colour. A narrower one wastes fewer pixels but leaves less room to hide mistakes. The right choice depends on how tolerant the content is. Soft, organic imagery forgives a narrow seam. Hard geometric lines, text and logos crossing the seam do not.

Overlap also has a direct cost in resolution. Consider two projectors with a native width of 1920 pixels and an overlap of 20 percent, which is 384 pixels. The combined width is 1920 plus 1920 minus 384, or 3456 pixels, not 3840. With three projectors at 15 percent overlap (288 pixels), the result is 5760 minus 576, or 5184 pixels. The more projectors you chain, the more of the total the seams consume, which is why the canvas has to be calculated from the real output layout and not from the sum of projector resolutions. We cover that calculation in our note on choosing canvas resolution for projection mapping.

Black level and projector matching

Projectors never produce perfect black. They leak a small amount of light even when showing a black pixel, and in an overlap zone that leakage from two projectors adds together. The result is a faint lighter band where the beams meet, visible whenever the content is dark. Most blending tools include a black level correction that raises the black of the single-projector areas to match the overlap, but this works by lifting the blacks everywhere else, which lowers contrast across the whole image.

This is one reason we favour projectors with strong native contrast for large shows and why the choice of a dark or bright scene matters. On a pale stone facade at night the effect is subtle. On a dark surface with a great deal of black in the content, it can be the most visible flaw in the show. It is also a reason to avoid overlap in places where the content is mostly empty, and to put seams behind busy or bright parts of the image where possible.

Where to put the seams

A seam does not have to fall in the geometric middle between two projectors' coverage areas, and often should not. Architecture gives natural places to hide a join: a recess, a change of plane, a column, a strong horizontal band. Aligning an overlap with one of these means the eye reads the transition as part of the building. A seam running through the middle of a large flat wall, or through a window, is much harder to hide, because there is nothing to distract from it.

Seams also interact with the surface. A blend that is perfect on a flat wall can fail on a projecting element or a deep recess, because the two projectors see that part of the surface from different angles and the pixels no longer coincide. Where a seam must cross relief, we try to keep it on the same plane or move it. This is one of the reasons the position of each projector matters so much, which we discuss in projector placement for architectural projection mapping. Where there is a choice, previsualisation is the cheapest place to find these problems before the trucks arrive.

Blending assumes that the projectors in a group behave alike. In practice, units of the same model differ slightly in brightness and colour, and the differences grow as lamps or laser sources age. If one unit is noticeably warmer than its neighbour, the blend will be level in brightness but visibly off in colour across the seam.

  • Use the same model, ideally from the same batch, for any set of projectors that share a seam.
  • Use the same lens, or lenses with the same characteristics, so that image size and focus behave consistently.
  • Set brightness, colour temperature and any dynamic contrast features identically, and switch off automatic adjustments that change picture settings by themselves.
  • Let every unit warm up to a stable state before final colour matching.
  • Match on a test pattern first, then check with real content, because a grey ramp will not show what a saturated red does.

Designing content that survives the seam

Content designers can make a seam easier or harder to hide. Fine horizontal lines that cross the overlap at an angle are the most demanding, because a tiny misalignment shows immediately. Large soft gradients, particles and organic motion are forgiving. Keep important text and logos away from seams, or ensure they sit entirely within one projector's area. Avoid pure black areas that need to remain black, and avoid very bright, saturated edges at a join, which reveal slight colour shifts.

The content team also needs to know where the overlaps are. We give designers a map of the output layout with the seams marked and a clear safe area for key elements, so the decisions about composition are made with the real structure in view. Testing a short loop of hard-edged and gradient content across each seam early in the project avoids unpleasant surprises during alignment.

A blending checklist

ItemWhat to confirm
OverlapPercentage chosen for each seam and its effect on usable pixels
Seam positionPlaced on architectural breaks, away from relief and key content
AlignmentWarping completed before blending is applied
Black levelCorrection applied and its contrast cost accepted
ProjectorsSame model, lens and settings, warmed up before matching
ContentText and fine lines kept off seams; test loop played across each one
RecheckBlend inspected again on the final night, at the audience position

A blend that looks flawless from the projector position can look different from the audience area, especially on textured surfaces or when seams cross uneven relief. The last check should always be made from where people will actually stand, at the actual time of the show, with the real content. Light levels, the surrounding environment and the surface itself all change after dark, and small adjustments at that point are normal. Plan time for them in the schedule, as described in our show-week schedule.
