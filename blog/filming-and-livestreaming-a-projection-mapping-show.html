Filming and livestreaming a projection mapping show

How to film and livestream a projection mapping show: camera positions, settings for projected light, upload and audio planning, and a practical checklist.

BY FAHAD KHAN TAREENNOVEMBER 3, 20267 MIN READ
AKS FIELD NOTES← All articlesView projects ↗

A projection mapping show lasts minutes, but the footage of it is used for years: on websites, in tenders, on social channels and in the client's own archive. Many productions treat filming as an afterthought, hand a camera operator a spot at the back of the crowd, and then discover that the recording shows banding, blown highlights, a crooked building and sound that does not match the picture. Filming a projected image at night is a technical problem of its own. At AKS we plan the documentation and any livestream alongside the show itself, because decisions made for the live audience, such as projector position, crowd zones and timing, directly shape what a camera can capture. This note covers the planning questions we ask, the camera settings that matter, and a checklist you can adapt.

Decide what the recording is for

A recording that has to do everything usually does nothing well. Before choosing positions, agree the purpose. A highlight film for marketing needs clean, wide, well-exposed pictures plus close detail shots. A livestream needs a continuous, reliable feed that tolerates mistakes. A record for the building owner or authority may only need proof of what was shown. A client who wants a vertical clip for social channels will need a different crop from one who wants a cinema-style wide shot.

Facades are often tall and narrow, or very wide, so they rarely match a standard 16:9 frame. If the vertical format matters, say so early. It changes where the camera stands, which lens it uses and how much dark space around the building ends up in the picture. We prefer to shoot a wide frame with room to crop rather than to guess and be locked in.

Choose camera positions with the show, not after it

The best camera position is rarely the best audience position, and the two compete for the same ground. The straight-on view from the main viewing area is the most honest, because it shows what the audience sees and keeps the building geometry correct. We usually reserve a clear, level spot there, slightly raised if the site allows, so that heads in the crowd do not cut across the bottom of the frame. Our note on audience sightlines explains how viewing zones are set, and the camera platform should be planned as part of that map.

A second position at an angle adds depth, because mapped content that follows relief, fins or columns looks more dimensional when seen off-axis. Be aware that the projection is aligned for a specific viewpoint, and a camera far from it will see some distortion. That is not a fault in the show, but the client should expect it. A third, closer position with a longer lens is useful for details such as a single window or ornament lighting up.

Elevated views from a rooftop, a neighbouring building or a lift platform give a strong overview, but they need safe access, permission from the owner and often a risk assessment. Aerial footage from a drone is a separate matter. It needs its own approvals and should be planned with the same care we describe in our note on combining drones and projection mapping. Our note on permissions for public shows lists the kind of approvals worth checking for any filming equipment on public ground.

Camera settings that matter for projected light

A projected image is a bright, saturated light source against a dark surround, which is a difficult scene for any sensor. These are the habits we rely on, and they apply broadly across camera brands.

  • Lock exposure and white balance. Automatic modes chase every scene change and produce pumping brightness and shifting colour. Set both manually during the rehearsal, with the content playing.
  • Expose to protect highlights. A clipped bright area cannot be recovered. A slightly darker file with detail in the brightest content is easier to grade than one with burnt-out whites.
  • Match shutter to the playback. Projectors and screens refresh at set rates, and a mismatched shutter speed can show as dark bands or flicker in the recording, even when the live audience sees nothing wrong. Test a few shutter values on the night of the rehearsal and watch the footage on a monitor, not the camera's small screen.
  • Keep the frame rate consistent. Record at a rate that suits the delivery format and, where possible, relate it to the playback rate of the show. We discuss playback rates in our note on codecs and frame rates.
  • Use manual focus. Autofocus often hunts on a dark building. Focus on a bright architectural edge during rehearsal and tape the ring or lock it.
  • Be careful with noise. Raising sensitivity brightens the dark areas but adds grain. A fast lens and a steady tripod often serve better than a high sensitivity setting.

Record in the highest quality the camera and storage allow, and keep a second camera running as a safety. A recording is a one-take event: the show cannot be repeated for the lens.

Livestreaming: the extra risks

A livestream adds a chain of dependencies on top of the show: cameras, a switcher or encoder, an internet connection, and a platform. Any weak link ends the broadcast, and the audience online has no patience for a frozen picture. The points we raise most often are these.

First, the connection. Venue Wi-Fi and shared mobile networks are unreliable when thousands of people nearby are all using their phones. A dedicated wired link, or more than one independent connection bonded or ready to switch, reduces the risk. Test the upload at the same hour as the show, with a crowd, not the afternoon before.

Second, delay. A livestream will arrive behind real time by some seconds, and the amount varies by platform and settings. That matters if the online audience is expected to react to something the live crowd sees, or if a presenter on stage is speaking to both groups. Tell the client in advance and do not promise live interaction unless it has been tested.

Third, a fallback. Prepare a holding slate and a short loop of recorded footage that the operator can cut to if the feed drops, as you would plan a backup for the show itself. Our note on backup and redundancy applies equally to the broadcast chain.

Sound is half the recording

A camera microphone at the back of a crowd records wind, chatter and an echo of the loudspeakers arriving a fraction of a second late. For a recording that feels like the show, take a clean feed from the audio desk and mix it with some ambient sound for atmosphere. Check that the picture and sound line up, since the live audience hears sound that has travelled through the air, and a feed taken from the desk does not carry the same delay. We cover this timing in our note on outdoor sound and on syncing sound and visuals. Agree who owns the audio feed before show week, and who is allowed to touch it.

A filming and livestream checklist

  • The purpose, audience and output formats of the recording are agreed in writing.
  • Camera positions are marked on the site plan and kept clear of the crowd.
  • Any rooftop, lift or drone position has permission and safe access.
  • Exposure, white balance and focus are set manually and tested with the real content.
  • Shutter speed is checked on a monitor for banding and flicker.
  • A second camera records as a backup.
  • The audio feed from the desk is arranged, and sound is aligned with the picture.
  • For a livestream, the upload is tested at show time with a crowd present, with a second connection available.
  • A holding slate and a fallback loop are ready for the operator.
  • Files are copied to two drives before anyone leaves the site.

Good documentation is planned, not rescued.
