Sound for outdoor projection mapping shows

Why audio delay, speaker placement, venue limits and sync decide whether an outdoor projection mapping show feels cinematic or falls apart for the crowd.

BY FAHAD KHAN TAREENOCTOBER 15, 20266 MIN READ
AKS FIELD NOTES← All articlesView projects ↗

Audience members remember a projection mapping show as a single experience, but they judge it with two senses that behave very differently outdoors. Light reaches the crowd instantly. Sound is slow, directional and easily pushed around by wind, buildings and traffic. Many shows with excellent visuals still feel flat because the audio was treated as an afterthought, bolted on after the content was locked. This note covers how we think about sound for outdoor projection shows and what a production manager should ask for early.

Why outdoor sound is a different problem

Indoors, walls contain the sound and the room does much of the work. Outdoors there is no ceiling, no controlled reverberation and no boundary, so the sound system has to deliver level and clarity to every viewing position against whatever the site throws at it: road noise, generators, crowd chatter, nearby buildings and wind. Hard facades can also return reflections that smear detail, especially on speech and sharp percussive hits.

The audience is also spread out. A projection show on a monument or fort is typically watched from a wide arc of positions at very different distances. A system that sounds good at the front can be thin and late at the back, and a system turned up to cover the back can be painfully loud at the front. Good design starts by accepting that one loudspeaker cluster rarely serves the whole site.

Sound is late: understanding delay

Sound travels at roughly 343 metres per second in air at about 20 degrees Celsius, a little faster in heat and a little slower in cold. That works out to about 3 milliseconds per metre. A viewer 100 metres from the loudspeakers hears the sound roughly 290 milliseconds after it leaves them. Picture, by contrast, arrives effectively instantly.

This matters because the eye and ear are sensitive to mismatch on impacts. When a building flashes on a drum hit, even a short lag between the flash and the thump reads as loose and unprofessional. On a large site the lag is different for every part of the audience, so there is no single perfect setting. We pick a reference position for the picture-to-sound alignment, usually where the main audience and the best view coincide, and design the system so that other zones are acceptable rather than perfect.

Speaker placement and delay zones

There are two broad strategies. The first is a main loudspeaker system placed near the building, aimed at the audience, with every listener hearing the same source. It is simple and keeps sound coming from the direction of the picture, but coverage at long distances is limited by how much level you can afford without overwhelming the near seats.

The second is a main system plus delay loudspeakers placed part-way through the audience. Each delay position is fed the same signal, held back electronically by the time sound takes to travel from the main system. Listeners in that zone hear the nearby loudspeaker first and the main system slightly after, which keeps level up at distance while the sound still seems to originate from the front. Delay settings are normally set with measurement tools and then checked by ear, because the numbers on paper rarely survive the real site.

Whichever approach is used, aim loudspeakers at the audience rather than at the building. A facade is a large reflective surface, and sound thrown at it comes back late as a muddy echo. Cabling, rigging, weather cover and safe public access around the equipment need the same planning as the projectors.

Design the track for the site, not for headphones

A soundtrack that works on studio monitors can collapse outdoors. Wide stereo effects collapse when the audience is spread across a plaza and no one is sitting in the sweet spot. Very low bass can be difficult to control and can irritate neighbours, and fine, quiet detail is simply masked by ambient noise. We favour clear mid-range content, strong rhythmic structure and dynamic moments that are bold enough to carry across the crowd.

Speech needs particular care. If a show includes narration, a spoken dedication or a name read aloud, keep it simple, leave space around it and avoid putting music and effects over the same frequencies. A reflective facade and distance both reduce intelligibility, so test speech at the back of the audience, not at the mixing desk.

Plan loudness as well. Venues, event organisers and local authorities often set noise limits or require an agreed sound level for public events. Ask about restrictions early, because a limit that is discovered at rehearsal can force a rethink of both the mix and the speaker layout.

Keep audio and picture locked together

Sound should be locked to the same timeline as the visuals, not started separately by hand. Typically audio playback, media server and lighting follow a shared clock, as described in our note on show control and timecode for projection mapping. If the audio is delivered late, or the content is re-edited after the track is mastered, the sync points between hits and visual changes drift, and it can take a lot of hand-patching to put them back.

We recommend locking the music edit before final animation wherever possible, and agreeing a frame rate and a clear start reference, such as a countdown or a marker frame, so that every department aligns to the same point. If the audio is supplied by a different supplier than the visuals, agree who owns the timeline and who has the final say on timing.

Weather, power and redundancy

Outdoor audio equipment faces the same conditions as the projectors, and the considerations overlap with our guide to weather-proofing outdoor projection mapping. Wind changes how far sound carries and which way it drifts, rain can interfere with unprotected equipment and heat affects amplifiers. Cover loudspeakers and amplifier racks appropriately, and place them where drainage and cable runs are safe.

Audio redundancy is cheap compared with the cost of silence in the middle of a launch. A spare playback device, duplicated files, a second amplifier path for critical zones and a clear plan for what the operator does if the main feed drops are all sensible. The audience will forgive a short loop of lighting far more readily than they will forgive the music stopping with no explanation.

Rehearse with the real audience area

Walk the whole site during rehearsal, not only the control position. Stand at the front, the middle, the back and at the edges, and listen to a full run of the show. Note where speech gets lost, where the delay seems off and where the bass becomes uncomfortable. Take measurements and adjust, then repeat at the time of day the show will actually run, because the ambient noise at dusk is rarely the same as in the afternoon.

Fix problems in this order: coverage, timing, then tone. A well-timed, evenly covered system that is a little plain beats a rich mix that arrives late and uneven across the crowd.

Sound is half the show, and it needs its own plan, budget and rehearsal time from the first brief.
