Permissions for public projection mapping shows

Who has to approve a public projection mapping show: building owners, equipment sites, crowd safety, power, sound and content, with a tracker to plan it.

BY FAHAD KHAN TAREENOCTOBER 23, 20266 MIN READ
AKS FIELD NOTES← All articlesView projects ↗

The first question a client asks about a projection mapping show is usually about content or budget. The question that most often decides whether the show happens on the planned date is a different one: who has to say yes? A building-scale projection is visible from a long way off, it lights a structure that somebody owns or manages, it puts projectors, cables and a crowd in a public place, and it often adds sound. Each of those touches a different authority. At AKS we treat permissions as a production workstream with its own schedule, not as paperwork left for the last week. This note explains the approvals a public projection usually involves, what to prepare for each, and how to keep the process from eating your timeline.

Start with who controls the surface

The most important permission is the one from whoever owns or manages the building or monument. Public landmarks, government buildings and heritage sites are often managed by an authority that has its own rules about what may be projected onto the structure, how equipment may be placed nearby and what may be touched. Private commercial buildings are usually simpler, but a tenant, a management company and an owner may all need to agree.

Ask early, and ask in writing. What the owner needs to see is rarely a finished show. It is a clear description of the content themes, the dates and hours, the equipment footprint and a statement that nothing will be fixed to or marked on the structure. For heritage sites the concern is usually physical contact and vibration rather than light, which is why we keep projectors on independent stands and platforms and away from the fabric of the building. Our note on projection mapping on heritage buildings covers the design side of that conversation.

The ground the equipment stands on

Projectors for a large facade are rarely on the building itself. They sit in a plaza, a park, a rooftop across the street or a temporary platform. The land or structure they stand on may belong to a different party from the building being projected, so there are often two separate owners to speak to. A projector position in a public space can mean a temporary closure of a path, a restricted zone around the equipment or a security presence overnight.

When we survey a site we record, for every projector position, who controls that ground, whether power is available and whether the position can be fenced. That list goes into the permission request, so each owner is asked about the specific place we want, not a vague area. It also ties directly into our work on projector placement for architectural projection mapping, because a position that is perfect optically but impossible to secure or approve is not a real position.

Event, crowd and safety approvals

If the public is invited, a separate set of approvals usually applies: permission to hold a public event, crowd and traffic management, security, medical cover and fire safety. These are generally handled by local administration, police and emergency services, and the requirements depend on the venue, the expected attendance and the jurisdiction. We do not assume any rule carries over from one city or one venue to the next, and we advise clients to confirm the current requirements directly with the relevant authority.

What we can control is the technical side of the safety case. That includes cable routing and cable covers where people walk, barriers around projector and generator positions, stable stands that are weighted or tied down, and a clear plan for what happens in strong wind. The last point matters because a show that cannot be run safely in poor conditions needs a stated cancellation or postponement rule, and the authorities will want to know it. Our guide to weather-proofing an outdoor projection mapping show explains how we plan for that.

Power, noise and light spill

Three practical topics regularly appear in permission discussions. The first is power. A large show may need either a temporary grid connection or generators, and each carries its own conditions about location, fuel storage, noise and fire safety. The second is sound. Outdoor audio can carry a long way, and neighbours, residents or nearby institutions may have limits on level and finishing time. We cover how we plan speaker placement and levels in sound for outdoor projection mapping shows. The third is light spill. Projected light that falls outside the intended surface can reach windows, roads and neighbouring buildings. Drivers should not be dazzled and residents should not find a beam in their window. Masking the projection tightly to the intended surface and setting the projector angle with care are part of our standard alignment work, and we are happy to show approvers a map of exactly where light will and will not fall.

Content approval

Owners and authorities frequently want to see the content before they approve it, particularly on government, religious, heritage and national landmarks. Sponsor branding on a public monument can be a sensitive point in particular. We recommend sharing the storyboard early and agreeing on what is acceptable before final rendering, rather than showing a polished film and hoping it passes. A change of logo size or a removed scene in a storyboard costs almost nothing. The same change after rendering costs real production days. For that reason the approval step is built into our storyboarding process, and the content timeline should leave room for at least one round of feedback from the building owner.

An approvals tracker

We keep a single tracker for every public show, and share it with the client. It lists each approval, who grants it, what they need from us and the date it is needed by. How to protect the timeline

The most reliable rule is to start approvals as soon as the date and venue are fixed, and to run them in parallel with creative work, not after it. The second is to give every authority a single, clear package: one site plan, one schedule, one contact person. Conflicting versions of the plan are a common cause of delay. The third is to treat the show date as dependent on approvals and tell the client so. Our show-week schedule assumes permissions are already in hand by load-in, and our guide to what to send a studio before a quote lists the site information that makes them quicker to prepare. Finally, keep a record of every approval, with the name of who gave it, on site during the show. If someone asks to see it at 9 pm, you want to hand it over, not search for it.

We have delivered shows at public monuments and landmarks, including Parliament House in Islamabad, the Pakistan Monument for the ICC launch and Minar-e-Pakistan in Lahore, and our advice does not change: start the approvals conversation on day one.
