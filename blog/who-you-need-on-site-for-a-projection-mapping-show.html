Who you need on site for a projection mapping show

The crew roles behind a projection mapping show, from media server operator to site safety lead, with a checklist to ask your studio before you book.

BY FAHAD KHAN TAREENNOVEMBER 11, 20266 MIN READ
AKS FIELD NOTES← All articlesView projects ↗

Clients usually ask about projectors and content first, and crew last. On show night it is the other way round: the people at the control desk, on the rigging and at the edge of the audience decide whether the show runs cleanly. A projection mapping production is closer to a small broadcast operation than to a screening. This note sets out the roles we plan for at AKS, what each person is responsible for, and how the team can scale up or down with the size of the show.

Why crew planning is a design decision

Projection mapping happens outdoors, at night, on a surface that was never built to be a screen. Equipment is spread across rooftops, lifts, scaffolds or a field, often hundreds of metres from the people who must trust it. Nobody can see everything from one position, so responsibilities need to be explicit. When roles are vague, problems fall between two people, and each assumes the other has it.

Crew size is not a fixed ratio. A single building with a handful of projectors and a laptop-based playback system needs fewer people than a multi-surface show with several blended projector groups, live sound, drones or a broadcast feed. What stays the same is the list of jobs. On a small show one person may hold several of them, but the jobs still exist and should still be named in the plan.

The core roles

Show director or producer

One person owns the decision to go, hold or stop. That person talks to the client, the venue and any authorities, and is not buried in a technical task. Without a single decision-maker, a delay turns into a debate while the audience waits.

Media server operator

This person runs playback: cueing, level checks, output mapping and the main and backup machines. They know the show file well enough to restart from any cue. If your setup follows the approach in our note on choosing a media server, this is the person who holds the keys to it.

Projection technician or lead

The projection lead looks after the projectors, lenses, focus, alignment and blending. Once the building is surveyed and the warp is built, this role keeps it correct as hardware warms up, settles or is bumped. They are also the person who can tell whether a visible problem is a projector fault, a signal fault or a content issue. Our notes on aligning and warping to a building and edge blending describe the work this role carries out.

Rigging and power

Someone must be responsible for how projectors are mounted, how cables are routed and secured, and how power is distributed and protected. This is a safety role first. Safe working at height, load ratings for mounts and a clear way to isolate power belong to qualified people, and the production plan should say who they are. See our notes on rigging and mounting and power and signal cabling.

Sound operator

If the show has audio, a sound operator handles the system, levels and playback, and works to the same timecode as the visuals. Sound and picture are locked together in the show file, but a person still needs to watch levels against the actual crowd and the actual site. For the timing side, read our note on show control and timecode.

Site and safety lead

This role manages the audience area, barriers, access routes for crew and any restricted zones around equipment. It links the technical team to venue security and local authorities, and it is where permit conditions are actually enforced. Our note on permissions for public shows explains what usually has to be agreed beforehand.

Roles that appear as the show grows

  • Content or technical artist on call: a person who can fix a clip, a mask or a render problem on site, without sending files back to the studio and waiting.
  • Network and signal technician: for larger setups with long fibre or network runs, many outputs and remote monitoring.
  • Show caller or stage manager: when the projection is part of a larger event programme with speakers, lighting, fireworks or drones, someone has to call the cues across departments.
  • Camera and streaming crew: if the show is filmed or livestreamed, that team needs its own position and its own plan. See filming and livestreaming a show.
  • Drone pilot and spotters: when drones share the sky with the projection, as covered in combining drones and projection mapping.
  • Runners and local labour: for carrying, cabling and guarding equipment. They save skilled people from spending hours on tasks that do not need them.

Communication on the night

Most crew problems on a live show are communication problems. We plan for this the same way we plan for projectors.

A single channel for calls. Use radios or an intercom with one agreed channel for show calls and a second one for technical chatter. The person calling cues should not have to compete with a conversation about a loose cable.

Plain, agreed phrases. Agree a short vocabulary beforehand: standby, go, hold, stop. In a loud outdoor setting short words travel better than sentences.

A written run sheet. Every role should hold the same document showing timings, cues, positions and contact numbers. When someone is replaced or unwell, the run sheet lets a stand-in start from the same page.

Eyes on the audience. Someone with a view of the crowd should be able to report what the audience sees, because the control desk often sees a clean preview while the building shows something else.

Rehearsal and handover

Crew need rehearsal as much as the show does. At least one full run, ideally at night and in the real positions, shows where a person cannot hear a call, cannot reach a switch or cannot see the surface. It is also the moment to rehearse failure: switch to the backup playback, replace a projector, and confirm who makes the call. Our note on backup and redundancy covers the equipment side, and the show week schedule shows where the rehearsal sits in the build.

Plan for fatigue too. Install days are long and show night comes at the end of them. A fresh operator at the desk is worth more than an exhausted expert, so where the budget allows, build in a second person who can take over a key position.

Plan the people as carefully as the pixels

Strong content and well-chosen equipment still depend on a team that knows its jobs. We have run shows on sites such as Parliament House in Islamabad and the Pakistan Monument, and the pattern is the same each time: the technical plan works because every task has a named owner.
