Projection mapping or LED facade: how to choose

How to choose between projection mapping and an LED facade: brightness, fidelity to the architecture, capital versus event cost, regulation and risk compared.

BY FAHAD KHAN TAREENOCTOBER 17, 20266 MIN READ
AKS FIELD NOTES← All articlesView projects ↗

Sooner or later, a client with a landmark building asks us the same thing: should we project onto it or fit it with LED? Both turn a facade into a screen, and both can look spectacular, but they behave very differently in cost, brightness, permanence and risk. Choosing between them is mostly a matter of asking how often the building will be used as a screen, and under what conditions. This note sets out how we think about the decision at AKS, so that a production manager or building owner can reach a sensible answer before commissioning anything.

What each approach actually is

Projection mapping uses projectors placed some distance from the building to throw light onto its surface. The building itself is the display, and the content is shaped to its windows, columns and edges. Nothing is permanently attached to the facade, and the equipment is typically installed for a show or a season and then removed.

An LED facade is a display built into or onto the building: modules, strips, mesh or media-facade fixtures that emit their own light. Once installed it is part of the building and can run every evening, in daylight if specified, without a crew setting up projectors.

The two are not strict rivals. Plenty of buildings carry a permanent low-resolution LED scheme for everyday identity and host projection for special events that need a rich, full-surface image.

Brightness, contrast and the surface

Projection depends on reflecting light off a surface, so the result is shaped by the surface colour, texture and the ambient light around it. A pale, smooth facade in a dark area makes a strong screen. A dark, heavily textured stone wall, or a street with bright shop signs and floodlights, reduces contrast noticeably. We cover the physics in more detail in our note on projector brightness, but the practical point is that projection almost always belongs to the night, and it is judged by how dark the surroundings can be made.

LED emits its own light, so it is far less sensitive to the colour of the wall behind it or to the surroundings, and it can be built to stay legible in daylight. The trade is that it presents an image made of discrete points, and the apparent sharpness depends on pixel spacing and how far away the audience stands. A fine pitch costs more; a coarse one reads well from a distance but breaks down close up.

Fidelity to the architecture

This is where projection earns its place. Because the image lands directly on the stone, glass and ornament, content can follow cornices, arches and window reveals, so the building appears to move, crack, grow or melt. That illusion is the essence of architectural projection mapping, and it relies on accurate alignment to the real geometry. Our work on heritage sites such as Mohatta Palace in Karachi for BankIslami in 2024 and Rohtas Fort draws on exactly this quality: the architecture is the artwork, not a backdrop for it.

LED can follow a facade too, but the pixels sit in front of or within the surface. The result reads as a screen on a building rather than light on a building. For some clients that is precisely what they want: clear branding, legible text, live information and consistent output. For heritage structures it is often not an option at all, because fixing hardware to protected fabric may be restricted or refused.

Cost: capital versus event spend

Without quoting figures, which vary too much by site to be useful, the shape of the costs is different. An LED facade is a capital project: design, fabrication, installation, power, structural checks, and then years of maintenance and eventual replacement of modules. Projection is an operating cost: each show or season brings equipment hire or deployment, rigging, crew, power and content.

If a building will host two or three shows a year, projection is usually the more economical route because you do not own, power and maintain a facade-sized display for the other 360 nights. If the building should be a screen most nights for a decade, the capital cost of LED may be spread across enough use to make sense. The honest approach is to estimate the number of display nights over the planned life of the installation and compare both models on that basis. Our note on what drives the cost of a projection mapping show explains the event-side budget in detail.

Content, flexibility and permanence

Both routes need content made for the surface, but the constraints differ. Projection content is built on an accurate model of the building and a canvas split across several projectors, so production is front-loaded with survey, alignment and blending. LED content is built to the pixel map of the installed display, which is fixed and known, so the content specification is simpler to hand to a designer but is permanently bound to that grid.

Flexibility also works differently. A projection show can be reinvented for a new event by changing the content and re-aiming equipment, and the same building can be a different canvas every time. An LED facade can run new content just as easily, but its physical character, pixel density and brightness are set on the day it is built.

Permanence brings regulation. A fixed LED display on a public building often involves advertising, planning, heritage and road-safety rules, and a decision from the owner and local authorities, not just from the producer. A temporary projection show has its own approvals, but they are usually lighter than altering the building. In either case, check the requirements early, and get them in writing.

Risk and operations

Projection carries the risks of the outdoors: wind and rain at the projector positions, dust, stray light, and the audience or a vehicle blocking a throw. We plan around these with redundancy and rehearsal, as described in our notes on weather-proofing and projector placement. LED carries the risks of any installed system: dead modules, power and data faults, heat, water ingress and the difficulty of servicing equipment at height.

The failure modes also look different to an audience. When a projector fails in a well-designed show, a backup unit can take over, or the image degrades gracefully across the overlap. When a section of an LED facade fails, the dark patch stays visible until a maintenance crew reaches it. Neither is better; they simply call for different plans.

A quick comparison

QuestionLeans projectionLeans LED
How often is it used?A few events or seasons a yearMost nights for years
When will it be viewed?After dark, in controlled lightDusk, daylight or bright surroundings
What is the building?Heritage or ornate, no fixings allowedModern, designed to carry equipment
What should the audience see?The architecture itself coming aliveClear branding, text and live data
How is the budget structured?Event spend per showCapital spend plus maintenance

How we help clients decide

We start with the building and the calendar, not the technology. We look at the surface, the viewing positions, the surrounding light and the number of nights the client expects to use the facade. Often the answer is a mix: a modest permanent scheme for everyday presence and a projection show for the occasions that deserve full spectacle. Occasionally the answer is that neither is justified yet, and a single well-planned event is the better first step.
